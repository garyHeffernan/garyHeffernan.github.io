“Back to the rough ground!”

- Ludwig Wittgenstein, realizing his perfect model had zero traction.

The investment world loves a detailed excel model. We love the “crystalline purity” of a spreadsheet forecast where revenue grows predictably, margins expand on cue, and anything that can’t fit neatly into a cell can be ignored. But anyone who has actually put their money at risk knows that the unexpected occurs with comical regularity. A big mess is far closer to the norm than the outlook contained in the most detailed excel model.

The work of Ludwig Wittgenstein drove this point home for me. He performed the most famous “strategic pivot” in the history of philosophy. In his youth, he built a system of logic that was as flawless as a sheet of ice. In his later years, he realized that you can’t actually walk on ice. To move forward—to get things done—you need friction. You need the Rough Ground.

The “Ice”: The Trap of Perfection

In his early masterpiece, the Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus, Wittgenstein tried to force language into a perfect, logical box. He wanted every word to have a precise, fixed value. He viewed language as a mirror of reality—clean, binary, and devoid of “noise.”

  • The Analyst’s Equivalent: This is the “Excel Reality.” It’s a merger forecast where synergies are captured with ease and culture clashes don’t exist.

  • The Problem: It’s frictionless. On a sheet of perfect ice, the conditions are ideal, but you can’t walk. You slip. There is no grip to push against.

Wittgenstein thought he had “solved” philosophy by removing the messiness of human context. Instead, he had created a system that was perfectly logical but failed to capture reality.

The “Rough Ground”: Where Value is Created

By the time he wrote Philosophical Investigations, Wittgenstein had realized his error.* He looked at how language actually works in the wild—in the “market” of human interaction.

He realized that words aren’t fixed assets; they are tools. Their meaning isn’t derived from a hidden logical structure, but from their use.

  • The Pivot: He moved from a “Correspondence Theory” (words = pictures) to a “Use Theory” (words = tools/games).

  • The Friction: Real communication is messy. It relies on context, shared history, and the “roughness” of social rules. This roughness provides the friction necessary for traction.

The Takeaway for Investors

When Wittgenstein exclaims, “Back to the rough ground!”, he is issuing a warning to anyone drunk on the elegance of their own model.

We see this constantly in markets. Consider Bill Ackman’s investment in Valeant Pharmaceuticals where he extolled the company as a ‘platform value creator’ akin to Berkshire Hathaway—a pristine model of efficiency.

Then look at the subsequent SIRF Investigation, which revealed the operational reality: a secret network of phantom pharmacies altering prescriptions to fake demand.

The map was perfect; the territory, far less-so.

There is nothing “underneath” our language or our markets that guarantees order. There is only the messy, friction-filled game of interaction.

The goal here isn’t to build a prettier spreadsheet. It is to see clearly. To reject the ‘crystalline purity’ of consensus logic and find the solid footing that only exists in the mess. I want my investments to stand on the rough ground, because it is the only surface with enough ‘grip’ to protect the downside.

*Wittgenstein torpedoed his own magnum opus, not because it was illogical, but because it was useless. He valued traction over theoretical beauty. If he had the courage to do that, then I have the mandate to make this blog my own patch of rough ground—a place to test theses that might be flawed, incomplete, or just plain wrong. The alternative is to skate on perfectly smooth ice, going nowhere.
