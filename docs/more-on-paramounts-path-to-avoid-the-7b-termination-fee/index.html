More on Paramount's Path to Avoid the $7B Termination Fee

× Date: Tue, Sep 1, 2026

On August 31, 2026, the Plaintiffs replied to Paramount’s motion to modify the stipulation and No-Close Order to require bond. Understandably, the Plaintiffs do not want to post a $1.88 billion bond. To avoid it, they told the Court that the No-Close Order is not an injunction. Their argument asks the court to rule directly against Paramount on the motion. However, as I argued in my prior post, depending on how the court rules on the motion, Paramount could be saved from paying Warner Bros. Discovery a $7.0B Regulatory Termination Fee. For background, please read my prior post.

The Plaintiffs’ Arguments

In the motion, the Plaintiffs make three arguments: Paramount was not “wrongfully enjoined,” there is no “significant change,” and only a nominal bond is warranted. I will focus on the first argument.

The Plaintiffs make the following points when they argue that the No-Close Order is not injunction (lifted directly from the August 31, 2026 motion):

  • Having stipulated not to close, Paramount cannot claim the Court’s endorsement means it may have been wrongfully enjoined.
  • Paramount failed to cite any case in which a court has ever accepted such an argument.
  • Audi of America, Inc. v. Bronsberg & Hughes Pontiac, Inc. rejected the identical argument on the identical facts.
  • Courts treat stipulations differently than contested orders, and the parties’ agreement is the source of the court’s authority.
  • Entry by the court, even drafting by the court, does not convert a joint stipulation into an injunction.
  • Paramount’s counsel’s own emails categorically denied the stipulation was an injunction.

The Plaintiffs of course are not trying to help Paramount, but their actions may incidentally do just that. The Plaintiffs do not want to post a substantial bond. The argument they make to avoid the bond centers on the definition of the No-Close Order. They say the No-Close Order is not an injunction. Instead, they say it is a private agreement. And it is the parties’ agreement itself, not the antitrust statute, that is the source of the court’s authority to enter it.

Paramount could be very happy to lose this motion. A loss that casts the No-Close Order as a private agreement rather than an injunction issued under the Clayton Act releases the Plaintiffs from the bond. It incidentally could release Paramount from the $7.0B Regulatory Termination Fee in the merger agreement, as I argued in my prior post.

Criticism and my Response

The strongest argument I’ve heard that I am off track is from Eriq Gardner, Puck’s resident legal expert and someone whose opinion I take very seriously. He argues, correctly, that a ‘Governmental Order’ is defined in the merger agreement as follows:

‘Governmental Order’ means any order, writ, judgment, temporary, preliminary or permanent injunction, decree, ruling, stipulation, determination, or award entered by or with any Governmental Entity.

The argument is that by having the “No-Close Order” entered by the court, the agreement is a Governmental Order. The implication is because it’s a Governmental Order, if the deal doesn’t close because of it, then the $7.0B Regulatory Termination Fee payable to WBD from PSKY applies.

I agree with him that he has the correct definition of Governmental Order as defined in the merger agreement. Where we disagree is as follows. In the merger proxy, on page 129, it states the following:

PSKY has agreed to pay to WBD the Regulatory Termination Fee equal to $7.0 billion, in connection with a termination of the Merger Agreement under the following circumstances: if the Merger Agreement is terminated by WBD or PSKY because the Effective Time has not occurred prior to the end date when the closing conditions related to regulatory approvals and governmental orders (in each case, solely as a result of any antitrust law or foreign regulatory law) have not been satisfied, but all other conditions to PSKY or Merger Sub consummating the Merger have been satisfied or waived.

Therefore, to trigger the Regulatory Termination Fee, it is not enough that the No-Close Order is entered by the court, but rather it has to be entered by the court as a result of any antitrust law (or foreign regulatory law, which doesn’t apply here).

In short, my argument is that the phrase in the parentheses above limits the Regulatory Termination Fee trigger to failures caused by antitrust law (or foreign regulatory law, again, not applicable), and that a Governmental Order entered not under antitrust but entered nonetheless still blocks the closing, but it just fails to trigger the payout of the Regulatory Termination Fee to WBD from PSKY.
