What Happens Next in Paramount’s Pursuit of Warner Bros Discovery

× Date: Sun, Jan 25, 2026

“Powerful writing should take one side and stick to it tenaciously, ignoring the other even though it may have merit. Objective writing is impotent.” - Dee Hock, Autobiography of a Restless Mind

I’m going to make a case for what comes next in Paramount’s pursuit of Warner Bros. Discovery. The future is unknowable, and this post is highly speculative and reflects my opinion. I won’t cover every angle and instead will follow Hock’s advice to take one side and stick to it.

Paramount’s Unsolicited Tender Offer Starts the Conversation

A tender offer starts the conversation. In the case of Paramount and Warner Bros Discovery (”WBD”), Paramount’s unsolicited tender offer put WBD in-play. By ratcheting up the pressure with a series of rapid-fire, all-cash bids, the WBD board was forced to put the company up for sale, triggering its Revlon duties1.

WBD subsequently signed a merger agreement with Netflix for its Streaming & Studios business (”WB S&S”), but not for the entire company. Paramount insists that it still wants to buy the entire company, and is looking to unseat Netflix, causing them to abandon the merger agreement.

But that’s not how this has to unfold.

In fact, Paramount’s efforts to date look an awful lot like the Charter / Time Warner Cable / Comcast saga.

What John Malone’s Hostile Bid for Time Warner Cable Can Tell Us

In 2013, Charter, controlled by Liberty Media’s John Malone, launched a series of unsolicited bids for Time Warner Cable (“TWC”). At each turn, TWC found the bids by Charter to be insufficient, claiming that the price was too low, the component that included stock was too risky, and the resulting entity was too highly leveraged.

All of Charter’s bids were rebuffed. The TWC board sought a white knight, and invited Comcast to bid on the company. Comcast accepted the invitation, negotiated terms, and signed a merger agreement with TWC.

Re-read the example above and substitute Charter for Paramount, TWC for WBD, and Comcast for Netflix. The scenario is almost identical. And both scenarios, past and present, feature the guiding hand of John Malone, who is now Chair Emeritus at WBD.

What Came Next After the Merger Agreement Was Signed

What came next after TWC and Comcast signed their merger agreement is most instructive for what comes next with Paramount / WBD. Recall, Charter had launched a series of hostile bids for TWC. TWC raced to sign a friendly deal with Comcast. It’s easy to think that Charter would have continued in their dogged pursuit of TWC by raising their bid and igniting a bidding war. But that’s not what happened.

Instead, Charter signed an agreement with Comcast aimed at easing regulatory concerns around the TWC deal. Comcast created a new SpinCo to be jointly owned by Comcast / Charter 67% / 33%, Comcast sold 1.4 million existing TWC customers to Charter for cash, and Charter and Comcast swapped 1.6 million TWC customers for 1.6 million Charter customers in a like-kind exchange.

Charter put TWC in-play, TWC had to find a friendly suitor, found one in Comcast, and Charter ended up with assets in a tax-efficient manner.

The Comcast / TWC deal subsequently collapsed over regulatory concerns. And shortly thereafter, Charter signed its own merger agreement directly with TWC. Charter ultimately got their prize.

The Parallel to Paramount / WBD

The parallel to Paramount / WBD is obvious: Paramount’s hostile bids put WBD in-play. WBD sought a white knight in Netflix. WBD will dispose of certain assets.

Where the parallel breaks down, slightly, is that WBD already announced a divestiture of a business that Netflix does not want to acquire. Unlike the Comcast / TWC example, the divestiture is unrelated to easing antitrust concerns; it’s simply a disposal of an unwanted business.

Paramount can pick up the unwanted SpinCo (Discovery Global Networks, “DG”) and merge it with their cable networks business in a Reverse Morris Trust (”RMT”) transaction. More on this later.

It’s the sequencing of the disposal of the unwanted assets that is different between the two cases, and it does matter. In the case of Comcast / TWC, the disposal came after the merger agreement was signed, and the interloper (Charter) negotiated directly with the winning party (Comcast), not the company disposing of the assets (TWC).

There’s a method to this madness. The IRS does not want companies engaging in sales disguised as tax-free disposals. Had TWC simply sold assets directly to Charter for cash, the IRS would tax that as a capital gain. No good. Charter first needed to force the issue by dislodging the sought-after assets under the guise of a regulatory-driven disposal.

How does this apply to Paramount and WBD?

Instead of being a divestiture for regulatory reasons, the DG divestiture is a closing condition of the merger between Netflix and WB S&S. It rhymes with the TWC deal. The disposal of the assets is not because Paramount wants to buy them (which would constitute a plan in the eyes of the IRS). The disposal is a closing condition of a completely unrelated deal between WBD and Netflix.

WBD will separate DG in a contingent taxable / tax-free separation, depending on whether the Netflix merger closes. DG is being spun-off (”The Distribution”) in connection with the Netflix / WB S&S merger (”The Merger”). The Merger is expected to close after the Distribution. The Merger might not close at all. If The Distribution occurs and The Merger does not close, then the distribution is tax-free. To repeat, the DG spin-off is tax-free if the Netflix deal falls through. This allows DG to pursue a Reverse Morris Trust style transaction with Paramount.

The DG spin-off closing condition has been memorialized in multiple SEC documents at this point, including the merger proxy filed on January 20, 2026. Paramount has had no substantial talks with the WBD board throughout this entire process, as demonstrated in multiple press reports. The spin-off of DG is happening, not because Paramount wants it, but because it must happen in the Netflix deal. Paramount and the WBD board have never talked about the specific projections of the DG asset and have had no discussions about what it might be worth. The requirements are met for WBD to pursue an RMT of DG with Paramount.

Importantly, WBD revealed their projections and projected valuation for DG. In short, the board thinks DG is worth $4.63 to $6.86 per share in a transaction like a merger or acquisition. This is the target Paramount needs to hit if they want to pursue an RMT with DG. And they never needed to talk to the board to figure this out

Now that the merger proxy has been filed, the door is open for Paramount to drop their hostile pursuit of the entirety of WBD and focus on an RMT with DG. Just like Charter dropped their hostile pursuit of TWC and focused on picking up divested assets from Comcast / TWC.

Paramount can pick up the SpinCo (Discovery Global Networks, “DG”) and merge it with their cable networks business in a Reverse Morris Trust (RMT) transaction

For Paramount to pursue an RMT with DG, it will also need to have assets that qualify for a tax-free separation. Fortunately, they do, and the assets are their cable networks.

The cable networks, housed in the TV Media segment, are better separated from the company. The cable TV business faces different headwinds than the broadcast TV business, the streaming business, and the filmed entertainment business. Paramount shares the same rationale as WBD in wanting to separate their cable networks from their more valuable assets.

The problem with separating the cable networks into a standalone company is that they are likely too weak to stand on their own. In fact, that was one of Shari Redstone’s justifications for re-merging Viacom and CBS in 2019. CBS, the broadcast network, was far stronger than Viacom, the cable networks; Viacom found a lifeboat in CBS.

The cable networks need assistance to float, and that assistance comes in the form of a merger with DG. But first, are there any impediments to a tax-free separation of the cable networks? Below are some clues that show there aren’t any impediments and the team at Paramount has already been planning for this eventuality.

Clue #1: TV Media Preserved Continuity During the Paramount Global / Skydance Merger

The structure of the merger between Paramount Global and Skydance was done to preserve TV Media’s continuity as an active trade or business, a requirement for tax-free separations such as spin-offs and Reverse Morris Trusts.

Here’s how it worked: when Paramount Global and Skydance merged, they did not initially merge the two businesses directly. Instead, both Paramount Global and Skydance became wholly-owned subsidiaries of a New Paramount, which I’ll refer to as the “TopCo”. As subsidiaries of TopCo, the assets of each respective business were not merged into or out of any entity.

After the subsidiary transaction with TopCo, the assets of Skydance were specifically merged into the Filmed Entertainment segment of Paramount Global, leaving the TV Media segment unchanged.

Since the TV Media segment was never merged into or out of any entity, it has remained operationally and legally continuous throughout the Paramount Global / Skydance merger; only the corporate wrapper changed. With the same assets, operations, and corporate form, TV Media will pass the active trade of business test, allowing its cable network components to be spun/spun-merged tax-free.

Clue #2: Paramount’s Silence on Discovery Global Valuation is a Key Ingredient for a Tax-Free RMT

Paramount has not engaged with the Warner Bros Board in any substantive discussions regarding the valuation of Discovery Global Networks.

Paramount’s silence on Discovery Global’s valuation is a key ingredient for a tax-free RMT. Tax-free RMTs require the relevant parties to follow specific IRS guidelines to qualify for tax-free treatment. The relevant guideline here is that the parties didn’t engage in price talks about the DG asset prior to its required spin-off in conjunction with the Netflix / WB S&S merger.

We discussed this above already, but I wanted to reiterate that it is a clue that shows us Paramount’s intentions. Paramount have made it abundantly clear, in no uncertain terms, that the WBD board would not engage with them. All media portrayals of this situation suggest the parties are highly antagonistic towards one-another. In fact, the board did not even contemplate the DG projections until one day before filing the merger proxy on January 20, 2026, well after any high-level negotiations with Paramount. It’s impossible to suggest that the two parties directly discussed DG.

Now What?

Paramount is unlikely to escalate the bidding war with Netflix for WBD. Instead, they will negotiate a RMT with DG. If the TWC precedent is followed, this deal will be signed in approximately one month.

How closely this deal follows the TWC precedent is less clear to me. If history rhymes, then the Netflix deal is abandoned on antitrust grounds. Abandoning the Netflix deal would preserve the tax-free nature of the DG spin-off, which might give us a clue to the WBD board’s thinking.

As well, the WBD executives might be happier to continue to run WB S&S as a standalone company without the cable network albatross in DG hanging around their necks, especially after receiving a hefty $5.8 billion reverse termination fee from Netflix. After a sufficient waiting period for tax purposes, Paramount could yet again pursue WB S&S, just like Charter and TWC.

Another iteration: HBO Max is divested to satisfy the concerns of the regulators. Recall that Charter / Comcast negotiated a divestiture package in an attempt to clear the deal with regulators. So that could be another way this goes: Paramount ends up negotiating with Netflix for the acquisition of a specific asset with high regulatory importance such as HBO Max.

One Final Point on Paramount

Like WBD, Paramount is a collection of valuable studios & streaming assets, bogged down by cable networks. As well, Paramount houses a valuable broadcast network, a key ingredient for sports such as the NFL. You can read more about Paramount here.

WBD’s assets always had immense value, but no suitors wanted to touch the company until there was a plan to remove the bad assets. And the removal was no trivial task given the deteriorating nature of the bad assets, especially as they’re encumbered with debt.

Paramount’s fortunes could follow a similar path once the bad assets are removed from the good. There are various companies, such as Amazon, Apple, Walmart and Comcast, who should have an interest in Paramount’s studios, IP, streaming service and marquee sports rights. As mentioned above, a combination with Warner Bros. could occur down the road. A more speculative idea is that Paramount is combined with TikTok US, also controlled by Larry Ellison, in a reverse IPO after the cable networks are removed, which is a subject for another day.
