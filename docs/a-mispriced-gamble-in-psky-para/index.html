A mispriced gamble in $PSKY $PARA

× Date: Tue, Nov 12, 2024

“You’re looking for a mispriced gamble. That’s what investing is. And you have to know enough to know whether the gamble is mispriced. That’s value investing.” - Charlie Munger

Introduction

Today I will walk you through how I see the opportunity at Paramount Global and why I think the class B shares represent a compelling investment opportunity. Disclaimer: this is not investment advice. Rather, it’s a window into my thought process. My goal is to share with you some of the things I have picked up along my journey as an investor. Hopefully you will get something out of it, and can share your learnings with me, too.

It may be helpful to discuss my investment style up front so that you understand the lens through which I see the world. One way I find opportunities is not through quantitative screens, but by looking for misunderstood companies. If you’re familiar with David Einhorn, he talks about this style a lot more eloquently than I do. He likens it to having a debate with the market. What I’m trying to do is find misunderstood companies where I think the market has the price wrong by a lot. Once I find a misunderstood company, I invest, monitor the situation, and sit tight. The degree of freedom I give my investment process is time. I have no control over when the market will sort out its view. Sometimes it happens rapidly. Other times it takes years. Once I determine that an investment is no longer undervalued–either because the market has come to my view and the investment is no longer undervalued, or because I realize I have made a mistake in my assessment–I sell and move onto new opportunities. Although this strategy sounds simple it requires a disciplined approach and a particular temperament that combines patience, stubbornness, and open-mindedness.

Steinhardt’s Variant Perception Framework

When you have a debate with the market, one helpful way to frame it is through Michael Steinhardt’s Variant Perception Framework. Roughly, here’s how it goes:

  1. What is the idea?
  2. What is the consensus view?
  3. What is your variant perception?
  4. What’s the trigger event to make money?

What is the idea?

The idea is to invest in the class B shares in Paramount Global (“PARA”). Paramount Global is a collection of valuable media assets including a rich film and television library with intellectual property that spans all genres and demographics, as well as the #1 broadcast network, a leading free ad-supported streaming television service and the fastest-growing streaming platform in the United States.

What is the consensus view?

I thought Bernstein’s October 2023 Coverage Initiation covered the consensus sentiment well:

*“Paramount is facing a triple whammy - (1) linear decline, (2) sub-scale DTC, and (3) a poor balance sheet. … [Content] Licensing tailwinds are insufficient to offset linear decline. Paramount has proven to drive DTC subscriber growth and revenue growth but without scale in content, churn will stay high and engagement low. We expect [Direct to Consumer] OIBDA to be negative [until 2027]. *

The consensus sees a company whose fate is tied to the decline in linear television viewership, who is too small to operate in a streaming-first world, and whose debt load threatens its viability. To round out the consensus view I’d add the following to Bernstein’s summary. Today, Paramount is controlled by a shareholder whose record has been one of value destruction.

What is your variant perception?

Here’s how I think about the company:

1) A better way of thinking about Paramount is its asset value, and two key assets underpin the margin of safety in this investment.

**2) Paramount’s streaming efforts are already bearing fruit and represent a source of valuation upside **

**3) The long-term, fixed-rate, covenant-lite nature of Paramount’s debt enables corporate restructuring opportunities and **

4) The stock is depressed because of actions taken by the controlling shareholder and the path forward from here is uncertain, leading to investor confusion

What’s the trigger event to make money?

The trigger event to make money is the least clear part of the Steinhardt framework, but also contributes to the creation of this opportunity. The opportunity exists because of the uncertainty regarding how the future will unfold. Most people are not comfortable saying to their portfolio manager or outside investors: “I don’t know how this is going to play out.”

I’m going to use another Charlie Munger phrase to make the point that predicting Paramount’s exact trigger event is the least important component of the investment thesis. The phrase is: “Invert, always invert.” Instead of asking: “What’s the trigger event to make money,” ask “what’s the trigger event to lose money.” An obvious answer is an event that causes the company’s assets to become impaired to a level below the initial investment value. The share price is so low today that I think the odds of this happening are exceptionally low. The margin of safety is just too large – a point I’ll return to later. With the downside protected by a large margin of safety, my view is that the upside will take care of itself.

Company Overview

Now I will cover the Company Overview before returning to the four main thesis points.

Paramount Global is a leading global media, streaming, and entertainment company that creates premium content and experiences for audiences worldwide. Paramount’s portfolio is driven by iconic assets such as the national CBS television network, CBS Studios, Paramount Studios, Nickelodeon, MTV, Comedy Central, and BET. The company portfolio also includes 28 owned and operated local television broadcast stations and the streaming platforms Paramount+ and Pluto TV. Paramount holds one of the industry’s most extensive libraries of television and film titles. In addition to offering innovative streaming services and digital video products, the company provides powerful capabilities in production, distribution, and advertising solutions. Paramount currently is a publicly traded company, and National Amusements (“NAI”) is Paramount’s majority shareholder on account of its holding 77 percent of the company’s Class A voting stock. On July 7, 2024, Paramount entered into a transaction with Skydance Media in which the two companies will merge to form New Paramount, and Skydance will replace NAI as the controlling shareholder.

Paramount’s content spans sports, kids, films, and news. Sports content you might be familiar with includes: the NFL, the Masters golf tournament, NCAA March Madness, Big Ten Football, Australian Premier League soccer, UEFA champions league soccer, and UEFA Europa League soccer. Kids content you might be familiar with includes: Spongebob Squarepants, Dora the Explorer, Paw Patrol, Transformers, and Teenage Mutant Ninja Turtles. Film content you might be familiar with includes The Godfather, Mission: Impossible, Top Gun, Mean Girls, and Star Trek. News content you might be familiar with includes: 60 Minutes, Face the Nation and CBS Mornings.

Paramount monetizes its content across four main categories: Advertising (36% of LTM revenue), Affiliate and Subscription Fees (46%), Theatrical (2%), and Licensing & Other (17%).

Here you can see the consolidated company results. Total company revenue of $29.7bn in 2023 represented a 1.6% decrease year-over-year. Total company revenue of $6.7bn in 3Q24 represented a 6% decrease year-over-year. Adjusted OIBDA of $2.4bn in 2023 represented a 27% decrease year-over-year but is on track to grow over 30% in 2024 to more than $3bn.

Although the company aggregates its revenues across the four categories I just mentioned, I find it instructive to look at the company’s segmented results, which better delineate the company’s routes to the market.

The company’s segments are TV Media (65% of LTM revenue), Direct to Consumer (26%), and Filmed Entertainment (9%).

TV Media comprises broadcast operations — the CBS Television Network, the domestic broadcast television network; CBS Stations, the owned television stations; and the international free-to-air networks, Network 10, Channel 5, Telefe and Chilevisión; (2) domestic premium and basic cable networks, including Nickelodeon, MTV, Comedy Central, BET and Paramount+ with SHOWTIME, and international extensions of certain of these brands; and (3) domestic and international television studio operations, including CBS Studios, Paramount Television Studios and SHOWTIME/MTV Entertainment Studios, as well as CBS Media Ventures, which produces and distributes first-run syndicated programming. TV Media also includes a number of digital properties such as CBS News Streaming for 24-hour news and CBS Sports HQ for sports news and analysis. TV Media generated approximately 68% of consolidated revenues in 2023.

TV Media revenue of $20.1bn in 2023 represented a 8% decrease year-over-year. TV Media revenue of $4.3bn in 3Q24 represented a 6% decrease year-over-year. Adjusted OIBDA of $4.8bn in 2023 represented a 12% decrease year-over-year, and is on track to exceed $4bn this year which represents greater than 100% of the consolidated total company OIBDA.

Direct-to-Consumer (“DTC”) comprises a portfolio of domestic and international pay and free streaming services, including Paramount+, PlutoTV, and BET+. DTC generated approximately one fifth of consolidated revenues. DTC is Paramount’s fastest growing segment due to its exposure to video streaming. After posting a loss of $1.8bn in 2022, the company has turned the corner and posted two consecutive quarters of positive OIBDA in this segment. OIBDA improved more than $1bn over the past four quarters.

Paramount has been investing heavily in this segment, and the losses from DTC feed into the consolidated P&L that we saw earlier. In the most recent quarter, revenue is up 10% year-over-year, and Paramount+ revenue is up 25%. Paramount+ subscribers are up 13% year-over-year, making it one of the fastest growing subscription video on demand services and the #4 global streaming services by subscriber count. In 2025 the company is guiding to its first full year of domestic profitability for Paramount+, with the international operations following 12-28 months behind. PlutoTV, Paramount’s Free Advertising Supported TV (“FAST”) service is included in the DTC segment and is already profitable.

Filmed Entertainment comprises Paramount Pictures, Paramount Players, Paramount Animation, Nickelodeon Studios, Awesomeness and Miramax. Filmed Entertainment generated approximately 10% of consolidated revenues in 2023.

This segment creates content for other distributors, and creates films for theatrical release. Filmed Entertainment is a tough business and despite being a huge loss-maker for the company in the recent past, has slowly gotten back to break-even and sometimes even shows profitability. Despite not being a significant source of earnings for the company on a consolidated level, it represents one of Paramount’s key trophy assets, something rare and valuable that can only be had for a significant price, which we will get into more later.

Now that you have a basic overview of Paramount’s business, I want to do a quick run-through of the assets that I view as high-quality, irreplaceable assets that underpin the margin of safety in this investment.

Thesis Point 1: A better way of thinking about Paramount is its asset value, and two key assets underpin the margin of safety in this investment.

Paramount Studios (excluding CBS Studios)

Paramount’s Hollywood Studio, Paramount Studios, is a trophy asset. When considering this analysis it’s important to remember what a trophy asset is. A trophy asset typically refers to a prestigious, high-value property or investment that is acquired primarily for its status and prestige rather than purely for financial returns. Think of it like a “trophy” on display – it’s meant to convey exclusivity. Today, there are five legacy Hollywood studios, and it’s generally thought that only two could change hands in the near future: Paramount Studios and Warner Bros. Studios. The other three are in strong hands: Disney isn’t putting their studio up for sale, Sony is looking to expand its presence in intellectual property, and Comcast relies on NBCUniversal across its video and theme parks businesses.

Here I am going to triangulate the valuation of Paramount Studios excluding CBS Studios based on 1. Transaction Comparables, 2. Prior History, and 3. A look inside Shari’s mind.

1. Transaction Comparables

MGM is an interesting transaction comparable in that it was sold for $8.5bn or 37x EBITDA to Amazon. Amazon saw the value of plugging MGM’s library into Amazon Prime Video, despite MGM not having the full rights or control of its marquee franchise, James Bond. Further, MGM was colloquially known as a “defunct” Hollywood studio in that it shifted its focus primarily to production rather than being a full studio with its own lot and distribution. The acquired MGM library contained 4k films and 17k TV episodes.

Now consider Paramount Studios. It is a fully functional studio with global distribution and owns a 62-acre studio lot.

I want to take a moment to contrast a production company with a fully functional studio.

A production company typically focuses on just making content, rents studio space as necessary, partners with other companies for distribution, and often relies on larger studios to handle marketing and release. Whereas a fully functional studio like Paramount owns the actual real estate with soundstages, backlots, and production facilities. It controls how movies get to theaters worldwide, has direct relationships with theater chains, handles marketing and promotion, all in addition to the production of films.

So what is Paramount Studios worth? Let’s start with the studio lot. It’s the only one still physically located in Hollywood, which itself is generally thought to be worth around $2bn.

What about the value of the distribution and the library? The value of distribution is less clear to me, but let’s assume Paramount’s transaction comparable MGM would have been worth more with distribution capabilities than without.

How about the library? Paramount reports its entire library, inclusive of the valuable CBS library, as one of the largest libraries of iconic & irreplaceable IP with 200K+ TV Episodes and 4K+ films.

At the time of the Viacom-CBS merger, the library was listed as 140k+ TV episodes and 3,600+ film titles. In Viacom’s last 10-K as a public company, it described its library as containing 3,300 films and a “number of television programs.” I take this to mean that Paramount’s 4k+ films are primarily attributable to Paramount Studios, and Paramount’s 200k+ TV episodes are primarily attributable to CBS Studios.

Given the fully-functioning nature of the studio and the similarly sized film library, I think Paramount Studios excluding CBS Studios would not trade hands for less than MGM’s price $8.5bn.

2. Prior History

Prior to the Viacom-CBS merger, Viacom entertained the sale of a stake in Paramount Studios in 2016. Viacom reportedly wanted a valuation of 8bn−10bn. The sale did not take place. At the time, the studio was losing money due to mismanagement and has since been turned around (excluding the one-time impact of the 2023 Hollywood strikes). It stands to reason that a now-profitable studio is even more valuable today.

Finally, since the 2016 sales process, no new major Hollywood studios have been created, the dollar has been debased no less than 20% (which supports a higher nominal price all else equal), and the pool of potential bidders has exploded with the growth in mega billionaires since that time (e.g. Larry Ellison was worth $49bn in 2016 and is reportedly worth over $200bn today). These three factors support a higher valuation for the studio than what was considered in 2016.

3. A look inside Shari’s mind

Shari has been around this asset for a long time, is advised by some of the smartest media and entertainment advisors and presumably knows what a good price is for her irreplaceable Hollywood studio. Earlier this year there were rumors that Apollo had offered $11bn for the “Hollywood Film and TV Studios.” This caused some analysts to speculate that Paramount Studios and CBS Studios were part of the proposed transaction.

Here’s what the Wells Fargo analyst had to say at the time:

At the time I found the report to be way off the mark. But, as a generalist going up against a veteran Wall St. media analyst, I found myself wondering if I had missed something. Fortunately, Paramount included some more information regarding Apollo’s bid in their recently filed information statement filed with the SEC. We now know for a fact that Apollo’s $11bn bid excluded CBS Studios and “Paramount’s other primary television studios”.

An $11bn bid from a financial firm that is generally considered to be disciplined on price is an excellent proof point to bolster my view that the studio is worth at least $11bn.

Funny enough, Shari wasn’t having any of this. It takes a willing buyer and a willing seller for a trophy asset to change hands, and Shari wasn’t willing to let it go for “a price even twice the $11 billion valuation proposed [by Apollo]”.

Paramount Studios is worth $11bn on the low end, and according to Shari is worth no less than $22bn on the high end.

CBS

CBS is a valuable property whose content is in demand across linear TV and streaming platforms.

CBS Broadcast Network is the #1 Broadcast Network for 16 Consecutive Years (for ‘23-’24 Season). In addition to in-demand news programming such as 60 Minutes, Face the Nation, and CBS Mornings, the company has sports rights to some of the most valuable sports content including the NFL, The Masters Tournament, March Madness, Big Ten College Football, Australian Premier League, and UEFA Champions and Europa Leagues.

And when it comes to general entertainment, CBS knows how to make hits. In the 2023-2024 season, CBS linear network averaged 5.59mn viewers each night. NBC stood at 5.01mn, ABC 4.29mn and Fox 3.35mn. When you add streaming into the mix from Paramount+, the CBS’s viewership totals dramatically increase. A good example is Paramount’s hit show, Tracker. The season two premiere amassed 15.7mn viewers over the course of a week. Nearly half of that total came from streaming. Tracker isn’t the only hit: eight CBS Original programs delivered 10 million or more viewers across linear TV and streaming in the 2024 fall broadcast season.

When it comes to sports, the NFL is CBS Sports’ most prized sports rights contract. In 2023, 93 of the top 100 most-watched live television broadcasts in the United States were NFL games. If you want to be in the business of distributing live television events, and you want to sell advertising to a national audience, then you have to be in the NFL.

Paramount is one of three companies that have NFL rights through the 2033 season. The others are Fox and NBC. Disney has rights to Monday Night football ending in 2032. Amazon has rights to a smaller package of games in Thursday Night Football which ends in 2032. Google has rights to NFL Sunday Ticket through 2029. Only the legacy media companies (CBS, Fox, NBC, Disney) have access to Playoff and Super Bowl games. The NFL has the option to terminate its contract with its non-Disney partners after the 2028-2029 season. The enterprise value of Paramount today is approximately the total value of its remaining sports rights contracts with the NFL, assuming no early termination occurs.

The NFL contract helps drive subscribers to Paramount+, reduces churn during the duration of the football season, offers a portal to other Paramount content on the service, and serves as a content cross-promotional platform (e.g. the new season of CBS’s hit show Tracker aired following the Super Bowl).

What is CBS Worth?

It is difficult to estimate CBS’s contribution to Paramount’s result. CBS results are no longer delineated in the corporate reports. Although they were initially broken out in the ViacomCBS results, CBS content is now distributed by the TV Media segment and the DTC segment, making it difficult to precisely estimate the value of CBS.

As mentioned above, CBS content is in-demand, so theoretically the value lies in the content and libraries, not just in its form of distribution.

One way to estimate the value of CBS is to look at its earnings in the pre-Paramount era when it was its own publicly traded company. Within CBS were four divisions; Entertainment, Cable networks, Publishing and Local media. Cable networks is Showtime and Smithsonian. Publishing is Simon & Schuster which has since been divested from Paramount. That leaves two segments: entertainment (CBS network & studios) and local media (television stations). In 2018 these two segments combined for $2.3bn in adjusted operating income, which I’ll conservatively call OIBDA to keep it apples-to-apples with Paramount’s current reporting structure.

2018 was a long time ago, and the media landscape has shifted since then. One way of estimating the trends at CBS is to compare it to another publicly traded company with similar assets, Fox Corp. Fox Corp is the broadcast and cable operations of Fox, excluding its content studios, which were sold to Disney. Recall CBS still has its content studios, including the rights to the IP therein, making this not exactly an apples-to-apples comparison. Fox and CBS both hold rights to the most-viewed sports property: NFL football. And we know that viewership averages are higher on CBS network than Fox.

The trends at Fox have been encouraging. Broadcast revenue has grown at a mid-single digit CAGR since 2018. The company’s market value is higher today than it was in 2018, despite being a linear television company whose majority of earnings are derived from a cable-TV ecosystem that is seeing declining viewership.

Triangulating all of the above: Between CBS’s historical results, CBS’s history of consistently strong viewership, and the trends in its closest competitor, I’m comfortable assuming that CBS’s earnings power has not declined since 2018 and is at least $2bn in OIBDA.

Fox used to trade at a discount to CBS when both were publicly traded. But at that time, CBS included Showtime in its results, which may have contributed to a higher valuation multiple. Today, I think CBS should garner at least the same multiple as Fox, whose business is exposed to cord-cutting given its earnings are weighted toward cable networks. Fox is trading at approximately 8x OIBDA in the public market. If Fox’s controlling shareholder, Rupert Murdoch, ever sold, he would demand a premium, so let’s call a private market valuation 10x OIBDA, which is the low end of any major media deal in recent history.

As well, when CBS merged with Viacom in 2018, its financial advisor, Centerview, recommended a range of 8.5x to 10.0x CBS’ estimated forward OIBDA of $3.3bn to justify an enterprise value of $27.5bn to $32.4bn. This is in-line with CBS’s then market cap of $18bn and debt of $10bn. Fox was the closest comparable at this time, and was trading at 8.8x forward OIBDA.

On a sum-of-the-parts basis, I estimate CBS is worth approximately 10x OIBDA or $20bn.

Margin of Safety Recap

So far, I have conservatively estimated the value of two of Paramount’s most prized, enduring assets: Paramount Studios and CBS. Combined I think the two are worth between $31bn to $42bn. This compares favorably to Paramount’s current enterprise value of $21.5bn. Our margin of safety has been established. Now let’s look at some upside optionality in Paramount+.

Thesis Point 2: Paramount’s streaming efforts are already bearing fruit and represent a source of valuation upside

Paramount+ has 72 million subscribers, is on a $5.7bn revenue run-rate, and is expected to achieve domestic profitability in 2025, with international profitability following 12-18 months thereafter. As recently as 2022, the division in which Paramount+ is housed, DTC, was posting near $2bn losses. Now the segment has posted two consecutive quarters of profitability. Results have improved from more disciplined content spending across the industry.

Paramount+ loses money today, so I’m not sure how to value it. Like any growth business, the value of Paramount+ lies in the future. Contrast this with CBS and Paramount Studios, whose value I feel comfortable with today. I see Paramount+ as representing free upside to the valuation of Paramount.

Paramount+’s 72mn subscribers contributed $1.4bn of revenue in the most recent quarter, which equates to $6.60 per subscriber per month, well below market leader Netflix’s $11.60 blended global average. If Paramount maintains its subscriber count and increases monetization to Netflix-like levels, you’re looking at $2.5bn of revenue per quarter less $1.8bn of expenses or $700mn of OIBDA per quarter which is $2.8bn per year. Throw a Netflix-like multiple of 27x on this and you’re looking at a business that’s worth $77bn. As much as I’d like to believe this, it seems like a stretch to me.

What about transaction comparables? Like cable TV distributors, streaming distributors are in the business of distributing content to end-users. The value is in the relationship with the customer, not in the content itself. Hulu is a perfect example: it’s a distribution mechanism; the underlying content is owned by the studios. And Hulu is the first relevant transaction comparable in the streaming space. As part of its agreement with Disney, Comcast is selling its ⅓ stake in Hulu for no less than a $27.5bn valuation, and potentially as high as $32.5bn depending on the results of the ongoing appraisal process. Hulu has approximately 50mn paying subscribers. So the implied valuation is between 550−650 per subscriber. This is lower than the rule-of-thumb used in the United States when another distribution mechanism–cable system operators–were being acquired at $1,000 per subscriber, but strikes me as reasonable given the uncertain nature of streaming profitability.

Notably, Hulu’s subscriber base is primarily located in the United States. Applying the same range of revenue per subscriber values to Paramount+’s 36mn US-only subscribers results in a value of $20 billion to $23 billion. These US subscribers could be valuable to someone like Comcast whose current strategy is to build a sports-focused, US-only streamer. As a sense-check, since 2020, the DTC segment has incurred over $25bn of expenses. On a “cost to replicate” basis, the 20−23bn valuation range implied by Hulu’s valuation is not unreasonable. This gives no credit for Paramount+’s 36 million non-US subscribers which could be valuable to someone like Warner Bros. Discovery as they embark on a mission to achieve scale globally.

As of today, I’m comfortable with not paying for the free option in Paramount+ and hoping that it one day delivers a surprise to shareholders by receiving a $20bn+ valuation in the market.

Thesis Point 3: The long-term, fixed-rate, covenant-lite nature of Paramount’s debt enables corporate restructuring opportunities

Paramount’s $14.6bn face value of debt is well-spread out with maturities all the way out until 2062. The weighted average interest rate is 5.17%. A few years ago Paramount took advantage of the low interest rate environment to lock-in these low borrowing costs. Since 10Y treasury rates have risen to 4.4%, Paramount’s market value of debt is less than its face value, which allows the company to buy back debt at a discount in the open market, which it has done opportunistically in the recent past.

When it was issued, Paramount’s debt was rated investment grade. Therefore it had minimal covenants. However, it did have one covenant that required Paramount to buy back its debt at 101 cents on the dollar in the event of a change of control transaction. Paramount subsequently nullified this restriction by receiving a debt downgrade at S&P. The downgrade makes it easier to separate assets from the company as well.

Because of the flexibility afforded by its debt, I suspect Paramount will spin-off its cable networks, and I don’t think they’re the only legacy media company thinking this way. Comcast announced on October 31, 2024 that they’re considering spinning off their cable networks. And Warner Bros. Discovery’s major shareholder and cable industry godfather, John Malone, revealed that talks about a cable network spinoff began in July at Allen & Company’s Sun Valley conference. A roll-up vehicle would be the natural home of the jettisoned cable networks, as it would give the new entity scale to run more efficiently and better negotiate with cable TV distributors. This type of transaction works if the legacy media companies like Comcast, Paramount and Warner Bros Discovery feel confident that their streaming businesses can live without the cash flow provided by their cable networks. Wall St. hates declining businesses like cable networks, and removing them from the legacy media companies would remove the anchor that’s pulling down their valuation multiples. If the legacy media industry together agree on this type of transaction, then the logic is there to support it.

It’s worth mentioning that the studios attached to the cable networks would certainly have value to an acquirer: I can imagine someone would be very happy to get a hold of Nickelodeon studios and its associated content. But for now I will give no value to Paramount’s cable networks, despite the fact that they generate cash and would be valuable to a hypothetical cable network roll-up company. Should a transaction ever occur I could envision a transaction like the recent Dish-DirecTV deal where the transaction consideration was primarily the assumption of debt.

Thesis Point 4: The stock is depressed because of actions taken by the controlling shareholder and the path forward from here is uncertain, leading to investor confusion

I could spend an entire day talking about controlling shareholder Shari Redstone and how her involvement has so far been negative for the stock price. I’ll spend a couple of minutes quickly recapping the state of play. I’ll be brief since this story received ample media coverage this year.

The story really begins in 2016 when Shari Redstone took control of National Amusements Inc. (NAI), the holding company with controlling voting interests in both CBS and Viacom. After a few failed attempts, she successfully merged CBS and Viacom in 2019 to create what we now know as Paramount. This context is important as it demonstrates Shari’s relentless drive to get what she wants, even if shareholders don’t approve.

Fast forward to May 2023, and we start seeing the first signs of financial pressure at NAI. Paramount slashed its quarterly dividend from $0.24 to $0.05 per share. This matters because NAI relies on Paramount’s dividends for cash flow. The other part of NAI’s business – movie theaters – is under pressure as the movie industry recovers from COVID. The debt at the movie theater business – NAI Entertainment Holdings (“NAIEH”) – is the primary reason that NAI is under such significant financial pressure. Further, that debt is collateralized by NAI’s Paramount shares, which puts NAI in a position of potentially losing some of their Paramount stake. The pressure was significant enough that NAI had to bring in outside capital, securing a $125 million investment from BDT & MSD Partners. At this point in time it is generally believed that Shari begins having private discussions with Skydance’s David Ellison.

Things continue to develop in early 2024. NAI kicks off an auction process, with Skydance emerging as the preferred bidder. Despite receiving interest for the entire company, including a $26bn proposal from Apollo, Shari remained laser-focused on the Skydance deal. During this time, four board members resigned (three from the special committee) and CEO Bob Bakish, who is believed to have opposed the deal, stepped down. A lot more happened during this auction process than what I just described. Various parties kicked the tires, and a variety of deal structures were proposed. But the only serious bid that would get Shari out of her mess was the Skydance deal. Another deal that got traction was Comcast’s proposal for a streaming JV which I will discuss later.

The final deal structure was announced in July 2024. It’s a two-step transaction where Skydance first acquires NAI for $2.4bn in cash. NAI has approximately $650mn in debt, so the Paramount shares held at National Amusements–31.5mn A shares and 32mn B shares–are acquired for a total consideration of $1.75bn. This equates to approximately $28/share if both share classes are treated equally. Public A shareholders receive either $23 worth of cash per share, or 1.533 shares in New Paramount. Public B shareholders have the right to convert 48% of the B shares to cash at $15 per share, with the remainder rolling over as B shares in New Paramount.

Predictably, shareholders are furious with the treatment Shari received, especially since she forced non-NAI shareholders to buy Skydance for $4.75bn. To add insult to injury, this purchase price was funded with Paramount shares at $15 per share.

From a governance perspective, this deal has a lot of red flags. Potentially superior offers for Paramount public shareholders were rejected. The public share classes received differential treatment, and Shari’s shares received yet even more favorable treatment. Recall each share is entitled to the same economic stake in Paramount, it’s only the voting rights that are different between the classes. The loss of board members during the process is problematic, as is the loss of the CEO who allegedly opposed the deal. The presence of a conflicted board member on the special committee – one who used to work for the acquirer – is another red flag that may undermine the independence of the special committee. Finally, the lack of a majority of the minority vote on this conflicted transaction suggests that the deal will be subject to an entire fairness review in the event of a lawsuit. There are a lot of reasons to believe this deal will be challenged in court. That’s a topic for another time.

In summary, a lot went wrong. And shareholders decided enough was enough and abandoned ship. And it’s hard to blame them. So that’s how we got here. The key question becomes: is the market over-reacting to these governance concerns, creating an opportunity in the stock?

Shari was getting bailed out whether we liked it or not

Before we move on from this Skydance deal, I want to highlight one passage from the information statement filed with the SEC that details the background of the transaction:

Also on May 23, 2024, S&P lowered NAI’s issuer credit rating from “CCC+” to “CCC”, stating, among other things, that S&P believed NAI would not be able to generate positive free operating cash flow and would not have sufficient liquidity to repay its outstanding debt without refinancing or selling a portion of its stake in Paramount. S&P also suggested that it could lower NAI’s rating further if it believed that NAI could default on its outstanding debt within the next six months.

Recall the NAI sale process began in January. By the end of May, Shari was still without a deal for NAI or Paramount. The outlook at her holding company was so dire that the rating agencies believe that NAI might lose a portion of its stake in Paramount.

Shari was in an incredibly weak negotiating position, and it seems unlikely that someone would just do her a favor by swooping in and acquiring Paramount at a gigantic premium to get her out of her mess. Anyone interested in Paramount might wait until she defaulted with the hope of acquiring control shares cheaply.

Another clue in the information statement filed with the SEC relates to the deal structure. At one point, Apollo and Sony considered buying NAI’s shares directly from NAI for $1.8bn, or slightly more than NAI ultimately received for their shares from Skydance. This would coincide with a full acquisition of Paramount for $26bn in cash. But Shari didn’t want that, she wanted a full acquisition of NAI. Why?

One possibility is that the collateralized PARA shares could not be sold (and therefore a deal for Paramount or NAI’s shares in Paramount could not occur) since they served as collateral for NAI’s loan at NAIEH. A sale of that collateral would require creditor consent. Put yourself in the shoes of the creditor. You aren’t just going to let the collateral walk out the door. At worst, you block the deal entirely. At best, you negotiate compensation for your agreement. Either way, a negotiation would require time that Shari didn’t have.

Enter Skydance. The Skydance deal bought Shari time. And it did one more thing. The deal guaranteed the NAIEH creditors could do her no harm, either by forcing her into default before Skydance closes, or by blocking a superior deal if it materializes. In the Skydance FCC application it was revealed that before the deal even closes, Skydance is willing to refinance the NAIEH term loan. Skydance saved Shari.

To recap, shareholders believe that Shari exploited her control position at the expense of minority shareholders. That might be true and is currently reflected in the stock price. What is not reflected in the stock price is that Shari may have found a stalking horse bidder in Skydance to buy herself time before ultimately conducting a proper value maximizing transaction for shareholders. Let’s review why the Skydance team fits the bill as the perfect stalking horse bidder for Paramount.

What’s a stalking horse bidder?

A stalking horse bidder is the initial bidder chosen by a company (the seller) to make the first bid in a sale process. Here’s why this role is important:

  1. Sets a floor price - The stalking horse’s bid establishes a minimum price for the assets or company, giving other potential buyers a clear benchmark and preventing lowball offers
  2. Due diligence advantage - The stalking horse typically gets first access to conduct due diligence and negotiate terms, in exchange for investing time/resources in making the initial bid
  3. Bid protections - To compensate for their effort, stalking horse bidders usually receive certain protections like:

    • Break-up fees if another buyer wins

    • Expense reimbursement

  4. Market testing - The stalking horse bid helps test the market by establishing a baseline valuation that can attract other potential buyers, who must then decide whether to submit higher bids

The term “stalking horse” comes from an old hunting practice where hunters would hide behind horses to get closer to their prey. Similarly, in M&A, this initial bid helps “flush out” other potential buyers and make them show their hands.

The Skydance bid looks like a stalking horse bid whether or not it actually is one. A floor valuation for Shari’s stake in National Amusements has been set, but the only way to break the deal at this point is to overbid for all of Paramount at a price higher than Shari’s average price per share of $28. Skydance got a first look at conducting extensive due diligence and even hired outside advisor Bain to go “line-by-line” through the business units. Skydance negotiated expense reimbursement as part of their bid. And they negotiated a $400mn break-up fee if the deal breaks. Finally, the process tested the market by surfacing a group of interested parties, 15 of which engaged enough to be named as parties in the information statement filed with the SEC.

What do you have to believe for the stalking horse theory to make sense?

  1. Shari benefits from Skydance’s participation as a stalking horse.
  2. Skydance is willing and able to act in this capacity.

We’ve covered Point #1 already. Shari gets her desired price, she has little to no regulatory approval risk, creditors can’t block the deal, she actually gets bailed out at NAIEH before the deal even closes, and she gives herself upside optionality if someone else comes along to break the deal. It would have been difficult if not impossible to start a bidding war without Skydance’s opening salvo given NAI’s deteriorating financial condition.

The answer to Point #2 is yes but requires further explanation

The Skydance consortium is willing and able to act as a stalking horse bidder

Skydance is a Hollywood production studio known for its co-productions with Paramount Studios including the highly successful blockbuster hit, Top Gun Maverick. The film studio is headed up by David Ellison, son of Oracle founder Larry Ellison. Skydance Animation is headed up by John Lasseter of Pixar fame. Skydance Media’s investors include the Ellison family, Tencent, KKR and RedBird Capital. Redbird Capital is the minority partner in the Paramount deal, and Larry Ellison is the majority investor. Redbird Capital’s founder and CEO is Gerry Cardinale, a former Goldman Sachs partner whose first stint post-Goldman was at BDT Capital’s office working alongside NAI’s advisor and investor, Byron Trott.

David Ellison is the perfect buyer for Paramount Studios. He represents a strong hand that will be a steward of this beloved asset. He’s the head of a Hollywood production studio who could step into an even larger role as the head of one of Hollywood’s five major film studios.

There is one thing about this transaction that does not make sense. Why does David Ellison want to tie the success of his Hollywood career to a leveraged Paramount whose business contains not just a film studio but a fledgling streaming service and deteriorating cable networks? It doesn’t make sense to me that he woke up one day and decided he wanted to be in these additional businesses. I do think he would agree to acquiring all of Paramount if that was his only path to getting what he wants: Paramount Studios. The undesirable pieces could be separated on his schedule since he does not face the same financial pressure as Shari. If he wins, he gets what he wants and figures out what to do with the rest. If he loses, he collects his break-up fee, and his theatrical partner, Paramount, ends up in much stronger hands. It’s possible.

What about RedBird?

RedBird is a private equity firm involved in media, sports and entertainment. High profile investments include Skydance, Fenway Sports Group, Artists Equity (Ben Affleck / Matt Damon’s production company), SpringHill Company (Lebron James and Maverick Carter’s consumer and entertainment company), the United Football League (XFL and USFL merger), AC Milan football club, and the YES Network (Yankees/Nets/WNBA Liberty Regional Sports network).

This gives you a sense of the types of investments RedBird makes. But it doesn’t tell you the whole story. RedBird is a savvy, financially-minded investor group. Let me use an example to explain what I mean.

RedBird, along with backing from Abu Dhabi’s International Media Investments, had planned to acquire the Telegraph Media Group (TMG) for 600 million pounds. The group includes The Daily Telegraph, The Sunday Telegraph, and The Spectator magazine.

Telegraph Media Group was previously owned by the Barclay family, who found themselves in so much financial distress that their bank had no other choice but to seize TMG from their control. RedBird stepped in and assumed the Barclay family debt for 600 million pounds and gained control of the asset. RedBird saved the Barclay family and the Barclay family successfully paid back the bank. However, due to the involvement of Abu Dhabi, the UK government blocked RedBird from controlling TMG. After having bailed out the Barclay family, Redbird now finds itself in the position of having to conduct an auction to sell off the various pieces of TMG. RedBird has already sold The Spectator magazine to businessman Paul Marshall for 100 million pounds and expects to recoup its original investment and then some by selling the rest of the newspapers.

In the Barclay family / TMG example, RedBird acted not only as a stalking horse bidder but showed its willingness to help a notable family who found themselves in financial distress. The situation would have been dire for the Barclay family if it weren’t for RedBird. I contend that they analyzed the Telegraph Media Group, concluded it was worth more than 600 million pounds, and decided to take a swing. If they ended up owning the assets at that price, they’d be happy. But they’d also be happy to conduct an auction for the assets from a position of strength, allowing them to garner an orderly sale of the parts to the highest bidders. It’s effectively an arbitrage play where RedBird sees the sum of the parts value and decides to act as their own catalyst.

Puck News reports that RedBird plans on selling NAI’s movie theaters to one of the larger movie chains, again acting as their own catalyst by putting a distressed asset in stronger hands and conducting a proper sales process. Finally, Skydance, like all Hollywood production studios, was ravaged during the 2023 strikes and made only $25mn in EBITDA versus $126mn the year prior. The $400mn break fee would be a non-dilutive source of financing that could go a long way to plugging the financial gap caused by the Hollywood strikes.

Given RedBird’s history, I suspect they would at least entertain the option of disposing of certain of Paramount’s assets at a profit if given the opportunity.

Future Paths

As mentioned earlier, the path forward from here is uncertain, leading to investor confusion. I count myself among the confused. Where might we go from here? I won’t spend any time covering the path where the Skydance deal is challenged by lawsuits since the result would be a Paramount without Skydance, whose valuation we have already determined above. The two futures I see, broadly speaking, are 1) Skydance closes the deal and New Paramount is born and 2) New suitors reveal themselves for some or all of Paramount or New Paramount.

Future path #1: Skydance closes the deal and New Paramount is born

Here’s how Skydance sees the capital structure and earnings power of New Paramount, the term used for the combined companies. There are a few ways to think about what the business might be worth.

  1. We can start with the low-end of my valuation range for Paramount of $31bn. To that we have added a production studio valued at $4.75bn. So we’re looking at a $35bn enterprise value. Add $4.7bn of cash and subtract $15.5bn of debt and you get an equity value of $25bn. The new share count is 1.1bn, so the implied price per share is $22 which compares favorably to today’s $11 share price.
  2. Here’s another perspective on valuation: Larry Ellison, one of the world’s wealthiest and most successful businessmen, is paying $15 per share for New Paramount and aims to acquire a 70% stake. Given his access to detailed due diligence and the fact his team will control the company, it’s hard to believe he would make this investment if the shares were truly worth less than $15. This creates a logical valuation floor. However, this same control position presents a potential ceiling on returns – once Skydance owns both voting control and the majority of shares, they could choose to take the remaining public shares (the ‘minority stub’) private at $15. So while Skydance’s involvement provides downside protection at $15, it might also cap the upside at that same price. This dynamic creates an interesting margin of safety for investors buying significantly below $15, even if it might limit the maximum potential return. I’ll also point out that Skydance is set to receive $30.50 strike warrants on New Paramount which at least points to a scenario where they get the stock price higher instead of simply taking the company private at $15.
  3. Finally, Skydance projects $4.1bn of OIBDA in 2026. With the company on solid footing and earnings steadily growing, assume the combined company is sold to a strategic at that point in time and the transaction multiple is a median media transaction multiple of 12x. This equates to a $35 share price (adjusted for full exercise of Skydance’s $30.50 strike warrants).

It’s too early to nail down a point estimate of New Paramount’s valuation. Upon announcing the deal, the Skydance team provided a vision of New Paramount to investors, but the details were scant. And since Skydance is not a large strategic, they are basically just swapping places with Shari Redstone, i.e. they don’t benefit from the kind of synergies that a large strategic would realize. Skydance does unify some of the rights to their co-productions with Paramount, which is a positive, especially in a sale scenario down the line. With the shares comfortably below $15 and well below my estimate of value, I’m comfortable ceding control to the Ellison family who will either get revenue and earnings growing once more, unlock the sum-of-the-parts value, or sell the entire company to a large strategic.

This quote from Barry Diller sums it up nicely: “There’s no question Paramount has been mismanaged for many, many years, probably going back to Philippe, whatever … Mr. Dauman, or whatever his actual, real name was. If you go back and see how it was mismanaged and the company is still breathing, it’s a great opportunity. And the fact that [the Ellisons have] actually been able…to buy it at such a low value, I would think that you’d have to be not really very good to pull it off.”

Future Path #2: New suitors reveal themselves for some or all of Paramount or New Paramount

Walt Disney’s well-known sketch is a good way to think about content companies. You’ll see at the center of the company are the Studios, which own the content and intellectual property. The IP is then monetized across a variety of channels such as Films, TV and Theme Parks, and importantly, each channel serves to reinforce the value of the IP.

A company like Paramount doesn’t have the same opportunity to exploit its content across a variety of large channels as does its larger competitors. This makes it a ripe acquisition target. Between streaming, theme parks, and general merchandise, there are a variety of competitors that would benefit from owning Paramount’s IP, and because their channels are so vast, could afford to pay a high price to get their hands on it.

To get a premium price for any asset, you want an auction. And an auction takes only two suitors. The perfect example of this is the auction for 21st Century Fox. Disney originally agreed to pay $52.4bn for the assets, but after getting into a bidding war with Comcast, ultimately had to cough up $71.3bn.

Which companies might have an interest in acquiring the key assets of Paramount (or New Paramount)?

Netflix

Media reports suggest Netflix has been interested in acquiring Paramount Studios. They would desire the studio lot and the content library at both Paramount Studios and CBS studios. Movie theaters such as AMC won’t show Netflix films in theaters: an acquisition of Paramount might solve that problem for them. Access to broad theatrical distribution would also attract marquee talent to Netflix who demand a full theatrical release of their films. An acquisition of New Paramount might appeal to Netflix as well: Skydance Animation has a multi-year agreement with Netflix to develop and produce animated movies to be released directly on Netflix. Finally, they would benefit from the NFL contract since it would bolster their fledgling ad-supported streaming tier. They might even want Pluto TV to get access to a captive FAST window to monetize older content away from core Netflix.

Comcast

Comcast has also looked at deals with Paramount in the past and they are partners with Paramount in SkyShowtime, a JV which combines programming from Peacock and Paramount+ services as well as Sky Studios. SkyShowtime is available in parts of Europe.

From the Skydance transaction summary we know that in January of 2024, Comcast discussed at a high level a possible acquisition of Paramount by Comcast and a potential JV involving a combination of the companies’ streaming platforms. Subsequently, Brian Roberts, Comcast’s CEO, said he was only interested in exploring a potential license for Paramount+ content for a licensing fee. Then in February the proposal morphed back to a Streaming JV subject to Comcast having majority control of any such JV. The Paramount board found the Streaming JV appealing enough that they were willing to consider it as long as it didn’t impede any other transactions involving all of Paramount. In March, Comcast asked for a substantial cash contribution to fund the streaming JV. Paramount subsequently granted exclusivity to Skydance, and we didn’t hear about the Streaming JV again in the context of the Paramount transaction.

One speculation of mine is that the cash contribution that will arrive alongside the closing of the Skydance deal is to fund the Streaming JV with Comcast. Today, Skydance says the cash is for debt reduction, but I can’t help but wonder: if Paramount’s debt situation was so dire, why not raise the money now instead of waiting for the Skydance deal to close?

Apple

Apple is a wildcard. They are known as the least cost disciplined streamer when it comes to spending on content. But their spending has not resulted in much traction for their streaming service, AppleTV+. Apple’s streaming strategy is unknown at this point; they could exit streaming altogether, or look to bolster their service in a big way. Paramount, CBS Studios and the NFL contract might appeal to someone like Apple. As well, they are lacking in film talent, which would be provided either by Paramount or New Paramount (i.e. Apple could acqui-hire David Ellison to run their film studio). Apple’s TV studio heads are generally well-regarded in the industry.

Amazon

Similar to what I have argued above, Amazon Prime Video would benefit from Paramount, CBS Studios, and the NFL contract. Their programming is geared toward the same audience as Paramount’s, i.e. down the middle, mass-market content. Included as a perk in Amazon’s Prime subscription service, Prime Video helps reduce membership churn. Amazon Prime members spend more than non-members, so perks like streaming can further Amazon’s objectives of selling more merchandise to customers. As well, Amazon is the first tech giant to make a push into the NFL, and they could bolster their lineup by acquiring the CBS NFL contract.

Walmart

Walmart’s streaming partner is Paramount. Walmart’s biggest competitor is Amazon, who expanded their Prime membership to include Prime Video as a perk. Walmart’s response was to include a free subscription to Paramount+. As well, Walmart recently made a move to bring more of the connected TV experience in-house by acquiring long-time partner, Vizio, which ran into a snag at the FTC and is now set to close by the end of 2024. If Walmart wants to compete more directly with Amazon, it might acquire a streaming service such as Paramount+. It’s not hard to imagine a future where a connected TV allows for the direct purchasing of products from the TV screen. At the very least, Walmart stands to lose their streaming partner if Paramount+ is acquired by a different company, especially if that company is Amazon.

Warner Bros. Discovery

Like Comcast, Warner Bros. Discovery (“WBD”) also took a look at Paramount during the NAI sales process that kicked off in January 2024. They never made a formal bid, presumably because it would have been all-stock which would not have been suitable to NAI. The appeal of Paramount to WBD is obvious. The CBS broadcast network and locked-in NFL contract through 2033 would fill a gap in WBD’s shrinking sports portfolio. WBD has also publicly mentioned that their portfolio is lacking in Kids entertainment; Paramount fills that hole for them. March Madness would live under one roof. Folding in some percentage of Paramount+’s 72mn and growing subscribers to Max gives the company scale that they appear to be lacking. WBD lacks a FAST service, and Paramount fills that hole for them as well. Paramount’s digital advertising capabilities are substantially larger than WBD’s DTC advertising business.

Sony

From the Skydance sales process we know that Sony teamed up with Apollo to take a look at Paramount, but they never got a deal over the finish line. Sony has publicly expressed their desire to acquire more intellectual property.

Others

I am sure there are more interested parties that I haven’t considered, especially since fifteen showed up in the Summary of the Transaction section of the information statement filed with the SEC. Not all parties wanted all of Paramount though, some just wanted specific pieces. They might show up once again if certain assets become available for sale. Party G, described as a strategic counterparty, wanted Paramount’s studios. We know Party G isn’t Warner Bros. Discovery, Comcast or Sony. Here’s hoping Party G is one of the aforementioned strategic “giants” and we haven’t heard the last from them.
