The data center sector is experiencing an infrastructure investment boom, one that has received so much attention that even companies in old economy sectors like glass manufacturing are lavished with praise in the press. Just recently, Corning was the subject of a flattering piece in the WSJ. The article describes how Corning leveraged their advantaged position in fiber optics to secure billions of dollars of contracts with tech giant “hyperscalers”. Here’s the article for background: Why a 175-Year-Old Glassmaker Is Suddenly an AI Superstar

My hypothesis is that the data center buildout should benefit more than just Corning. The burgeoning demand for fiber optics should spill over to other industry players as well. One of those companies is Optical Cable Corporation (”OCC”), the subject of this piece.

OCC is a specialty fiber optic and copper cable manufacturer, and my belief is that they are positioned to capture some of the downstream demand in niche and specialty applications where scale isn’t necessarily a competitive advantage. OCC’s history has been in specialty cables for military/tactical, harsh environment, enterprise, and custom solutions. Where more ruggedized solutions are required in the data center buildout (e.g. edge data centers), OCC should be able to play a role. OCC’s new partnership with Lightera suggests they’re positioning to capitalize on the datacenter buildout.

The company is small and not nearly as capable as Corning of landing massive contracts from hyperscalers. But the good news is that they don’t have to. Simply winning an additional $10-$20 million dollars in sales would do wonders for the company.

OCC first caught my attention in 2025 when billionaire investor Jeffrey Gendell started accumulating a position. Rarely do you see a billionaire investor buying a stake in a micro cap stock. He has been on my radar screen ever since his name appeared on the shareholder register of companies in deeply out of favor industries such as coal and oil & gas drilling.

A lot of people use quantitative screens and look for cheap stocks that show low price to earnings multiples. Gendell’s buy is like a qualitative screen. I don’t know much about Gendell other than that he appears to be successful, contrarian, and value-focused. If he’s buying, then it’s worth taking a closer look.

The first objective is to see if there is any hard asset value that protects the investment on the downside. Then, it’s worth exploring the “what could go right” case.

Downside Protection: Asset Value

The balance sheet shows the net worth of the company on an accounting basis. Stated book value is $16.5M which is $1.86 per share. We can refine the analysis from here.

The balance sheet includes a line item for “Redeemable restricted common stock” related to a stock-purchase agreement entered into between OCC and its partner, Lightera. The net cash impact to OCC for settling this quasi-liability is capped at $2M. For accounting purposes, the reduction in shareholders’ equity from the increase in the redeemable stock is calculated as the number of shares Lightera is entitled to (642,900) at its redemption value of $5.1M. The mechanics of the redeemable stock suggest OCC will never actually be “out” $5.1M; the money they owe to Lightera is capped at $2M. More on this later.

For this analysis, book value is understated by the overstatement in the redeemable stock by $3.1M. Adding this back to shareholders’ equity results in an adjusted book value of $2.20. We can refine this further still.

Real Estate

Accounting values don’t always correspond to market values. Adjusting the balance sheet for “hidden gems” makes sense if the company has valuable, marketable assets.

In the case of OCC, they own real estate. The real estate is marked on the books below market value.

The two valuable pieces of real estate are in Roanoke County, VA (HQ) and Asheville, NC.

Roanoke is assessed by the tax authorities at $8.8M. Home to OCC’s headquarters, this property also includes a light manufacturing facility. At 148k square feet (”SF”) it sits on 11.28 acres of land. The tax value has steadily increased over time and is a reasonable downside estimate of market value.

The average price per SF for Roanoke industrial properties is about $74 per SF, implying a market value of $11M for this property. This is a reasonable high end estimate of market value.

OCC’s warehouse in Asheville is 52k SF, sits on 7 acres, was built in 1982 and last sold for $1.1M in 2016. The Asheville property is assessed by the tax authorities at $2.7M. In the case of Asheville, its tax assessment is well below the market prices of similar assets.

Today, in the Asheville/Buncombe County Market, warehouses of 10k-131k SF are listed for $91-$200/SF. A conservative estimate based on market prices is $115/SF, near the low end of comparable properties and well below the top of the range. This would imply a value of $6M, more than double the value assessed by tax authorities.

Roanoke and Asheville together are worth between $11.5M - $17M, well above the historical book value of the Property Plant and Equipment held on the balance sheet at $6.6M. Adjusting for the above results in an additional $0.55 - $1.17 / share in asset value.

Adjusting stated book value for the redeemable stock and the real estate results in an adjusted book value of $2.75 - $3.37 / share.

The adjusted book value represents substantial asset backing that minimizes downside risk. It’s helpful to think that on a per share basis you’re buying $3 worth of assets, give or take. Most investors don’t buy stocks for asset value. They’re interested in earnings and cash flow, and would rather apply a multiple to either one of those figures.

Here’s another way to think of it. At a stock price of ~$3.37, the market is essentially valuing the core business at zero. At that price you’re buying assets and getting the business for free.

The Lightera Partnership

In July 2025, OCC and Lightera entered into a strategic collaboration. Lightera is part of Furukawa Electric group, a multi-billion dollar leader in optical communications. The goal of the collaboration is to expand sales for Lightera-OCC integrated products & solutions in the data center and enterprise sectors, expand OCC’s sales growth, and create shareholder value for OCC.

Stock Purchase Agreement

Lightera paid $2M for 642,900 shares of OCC restricted common stock, which implies a price per share of $3.11 and represents 7.24% of OCC’s outstanding shares. This makes Lightera one of OCC’s biggest shareholders and they’re invested in OCC’s success.

The restricted common stock has special features unavailable to regular common stock holders. It is structured with put/call features. Starting in July 2027, Lightera has the option to put its shares back to OCC at the higher of its purchase price of $3.11 or the 10-day trailing average price of OCC’s common stock. OCC has a call option to repurchase the shares on the same terms.

From Lightera’s perspective, they get to participate in the upside of the stock and their downside is capped. They can always get their money back if the stock is below $3.11 during the put exercise period. Where it gets more interesting is what happens if the stock rapidly appreciates above the $3.11 “strike” price. OCC is only required to pay back the $2M if Lightera requests it.

Specifically, OCC would pay $2M for however many shares it can buy at that price, based on the 10-day trailing average price of OCC’s common stock. In this example, if the shares were trading at $20/share, OCC would repurchase 100,000 shares for $2M, and Lightera would have to dispose of the remaining 542,000 shares on the open market.

Given the long and deep partnership between Lightera and OCC, I’d imagine that Lightera does not exercise their put if the shares are trading well in excess of the strike price. Instead, they can sell on the open market and leave themselves and OCC better off. This instrument really is structured like a convertible note, and I see no reason why Lightera would reclaim the cash directly from OCC in a scenario where the shares appreciate. While this ‘put’ could theoretically create a liquidity crunch if OCC is struggling in 2027, it is more likely a structural safety net for a multi-billion dollar partner that is far more interested in OCC’s operational success than a $2 million exit.

The Goal of the Partnership

OCC reported $73M in sales in fiscal 2025 and is a micro-cap stock. Their history is in selling cabling for military-grade applications, think rugged cables that won’t break in harsh environments. Lightera, by contrast, is part of a multi-billion dollar organization in Furukawa Electric group, a leader in optical communications.

Lightera is now OCC’s largest shareholder and benefits if OCC’s share price is higher than $3.11 after the expiration of their 2-year lockup period. OCC benefits if OCC’s share price is higher than $3.11 after the expiration of the 2-year lockup period, and is better off if they don’t have to repay $2M to Lightera.

By looking at statements made by OCC on recent conference calls we can better understand the objectives of the partnership.

That wraps up the conference call that occurred after the announcement of the Lightera partnership. What was management saying before announcing the Lightera partnership? That might help illuminate the gap in OCC’s portfolio prior to bringing in Lightera.

It sounds similar to what he said after announcing the Lightera partnership. To me this suggests Lightera will allow OCC to further penetrate Tier 2 and Tier 3 data center at a minimum, and if Wilkin is sand-bagging, Lightera will allow OCC to penetrate some Tier 1 opportunities, either directly or indirectly.

The stock was extremely weak after the December earnings call. I suspect that the market was hoping for more clarity regarding the Lightera partnership and data center opportunity. OCC played it down, a lot. And the stock unsurprisingly dropped. See the big vertical drop in the stock price in December, from the high $8s to the $4s, coincident with the earnings release.

What I Think is Going on Here

To recap, a small $40 million dollar market capitalization company announced a strategic partnership with and an investment from a multi-billion dollar market capitalization company whose products include hyperscaler (Tier 1) data center solutions in the middle of a booming nationwide data center buildout. The stock initially went much higher on the news. Management refused to give any guidance on the opportunity, and even played it down a bit, suggesting it allows OCC to penetrate the same Tier 2 and Tier 3 data centers markets they were targeting before the strategic partnership. The market sold the stock back down to levels most recently seen before the strategic partnership was announced (although not entirely).

At present I have at least two coherent speculations as to what is going on here. The first is what is referred to as a bullet-dodge stock grant, and the second is NOL preservation with the potential for a full-company buyout.

Bullet-Dodge Grant

I learned the following concept from Mike at NonGaap. He calls it the dark arts of corporate governance. His view, which I agree with, is that there are subtle, legal and ethically questionable ways corporate boards and management teams manipulate incentives to favor insiders at the expense of shareholders.

One of these techniques is called a bullet-dodge grant. With a bullet-dodge, a company delays an equity grant until after negative material news is released. Management knows bad news is coming and waits until after the subsequent share price drop to issue equity compensation (e.g. stock options, restricted stock units, etc.). That way, the “strike price” for the equity compensation is lower than it would otherwise be. If the board targets a dollar value for compensation, and most do, then the result is that insiders gain more shares than they otherwise would.

Now, I’m not Mike at NonGaap, I don’t know him or talk to him, and he might quibble with what I’m about to say. I suspect the following grant by OCC is a bullet-dodge. Mike might actually have a different term for this variation, but the net effect is the same.

OCC’s Proxy contains the key piece of information:

Share-based compensation to executives is issued in accordance with the Optical Cable Corporation Amended and Restated Policy for the Timing of Equity Compensation Grants Under Stock Incentive Plans, adopted by the Compensation Committee and approved by the Board on March 29, 2016. Generally, the Timing Policy anticipates annual long-term incentive equity grants for executives (and other employees) to occur on or about the first business day after January 15 of each year.

Since 2016, it is accepted and known by company insiders that stock grants are issued in January.

Continuing with the proxy:

In accordance with the Optical Cable Corporation Amended and Restated Policy for the Timing of Equity Compensation Grants Under Stock Incentive Plans, the actual number of Common Shares issued to an executive (and other employees) is generally calculated by using the average closing price per share during the 20 trading dates immediately preceding the first business day after January 15, divided by the dollar value of the share-based compensation to be received.

The price is a weighted average of the trailing 20 trading dates immediately preceding the first business day after January 15.

All else equal, management would prefer a low stock price heading into grant season. And that’s exactly what they got after the beating the stock price took after the December 16 earnings call. Management had zero incentive to talk up the stock on the December call. It would go against their own self-interest. A quick and sharp drop in the share price heading into the grant date happens to look a lot like a bullet-dodge.

What strengthens this theory is that the “bad news” from December—that OCC is targeting Tier 2 and Tier 3 data centers rather than hyperscalers—was actually stale news. Management had already disclosed this strategy back in June. If the fundamental story hadn’t changed between the July surge and the December crash, but the sentiment shifted drastically based on management’s tone, it reinforces the idea that the price drop was artificial rather than a rational repricing of growth expectations.

If we did just see a bullet-dodge then it means that the stock market’s reaction to the July partnership announcement was the right one, i.e. the best days are ahead of the company as they capitalize on the data center opportunity.

NOL Preservation, and a Potential Buyout if the Partnership Goes Well

I’d consider what follows even more speculative than what I just wrote about the bullet-dodge. But it is consistent with the scenario I see unfolding.

OCC is sitting on a LOT of net operating losses (”NOLs”), which allow the company to shield future profits from federal-level taxation. The company has $672,000 of gross NOLs under Section 382 limitations, and an addition $13 million of federal NOLs and $2.6 million of state NOLs.

For NOLs to be preserved, specific rules must be adhered to, which I won’t cover here. One way for a company to lose their NOLs is to engage in a change of control transaction.

My speculation is that OCC and Lightera might first want to utilize some NOLs while they’re working out their partnership before a potential buyout of OCC by Lightera.

Consider the following points:

  1. The partnership calls for OCC to sell Lightera products. Lightera will use OCC as a channel to increase OCC’s revenue. OCC has not been profitable for years and only in 3Q25 did their operating margin cross into positive territory at 3.1%. If the revenue from the Lightera partnership is sufficient to keep OCC profitable, then OCC benefits from shielding those profits from corporate-level taxation. In turn, Lightera gets to participate in any upside from their stock investment.

  2. OCC has an NOL Rights Plan. Think of an NOL Rights Plan as a poison pill to prevent anyone from gaining a large percentage ownership in the company, one that would complicate the preservation of the NOLs. It suggests that management would prefer to utilize the NOLs instead of having a corporate raider or strategic acquirer go hostile on the company. The path to any acquisition of OCC goes through the management and board, and the Lightera plan to first utilize some of the NOLs is a good compromise that the board could get behind.

  3. CEO Wilkin is 61 years old. He has been a Director of the company since 2001. He was promoted to CEO from CFO after a margin call crisis befell OCC founder CEO Robert Kopstein in late 2001. Wilkin took over the reigns after a spectacular collapse in the stock price.

Today, Wilkin owns 13% of the company. The other board members are 73, 79, 76 and 65 years old. This is not a young group. All Directors and executive officers as a group own 23.8% of the company. If Wilkin could see an exit at $10 or above, he stands to make >$10 million from his 1.1 million shares. A sufficiently high share price in a buyout might be enough to get Wilkin to walk away from his $495,000 annual salary (before share based awards and other compensation).

Model

For illustrative purposes I’ve included a model. I would not anchor to it. The most important points to takeaway from the model are that FY25 revenue grew 9%, gross margin expanded to 30.9%, and operating and net losses narrowed to almost breakeven (with a positive operating margin of 3.1% in 3Q25 on +22.8% y/y revenue growth and 31.7% gross margins). Not shown is the backlog which grew from $5.7M in Oct 24 to $7.3M in Oct 25.

For estimates, I assume incremental sales from four categories (and no deterioration in the run-rate business):

  1. Product Resale which is Lightera products sold via OCC channels at 17.5% distribution margins

  2. Integrated Solutions which are Bundled OCC cable and Lightera fiber solutions at 37.5% value-added margins

  3. New customers wins in data center at 30% margins

  4. Existing customer upsell at 33.5% margins

The incremental revenue contribution results in ~18% revenue growth for the next two years. This would put OCC solidly in positive profit margin territory and sales would hit about $100 million, well within their current capacity footprint, since they claim to be operating at half capacity with $73 million of sales.

I included the model to help create a framework to understand what’s possible if the company starts executing on what they suggest is an exciting opportunity.

Another small-cap electronic equipment company, RF Industries (”RFIL”), recently saw their profitability inflect from negative to positive. That’s the kind of scenario I hope to see occur with OCC. RFIL achieved slight breakeven in the last quarter of 2024, and has steadily increased to a 4% operating margin. This is similar to what my model shows for OCC. The market rewarded RFIL with a 1.7x NTM EV/Revenue multiple. RFIL has historically traded at a premium to OCC. Even if OCC achieved a 1.0x EV/Revenue multiple, on today’s sales of $73M, that’s 42% upside. If they hit $100M in sales, that’s >100% upside. If the EV/Sales multiple expands, like RFIL, the upside is even more significant. The goal isn’t to nail a point estimate for the upside; rather it’s to show that there is a positive skew if OCC delivers on their Lightera partnership.

AI Summary

While giants like Corning capture headlines with massive hyperscaler contracts, I expect the current AI-driven infrastructure boom to spill over into more specialized companies. Optical Cable Corporation—a micro-cap manufacturer of high-performance fiber optic and copper cabling—is uniquely positioned to capture demand in Tier 2 and Tier 3 data centers, as well as niche “harsh environment” applications (military, industrial, and broadcast) where specialized, ruggedized solutions are required.

Key Points

  • Asset-Backed Downside Protection: The stock trades near its liquidation value. Adjusted for “hidden gems”—specifically real estate in Roanoke, VA, and Asheville, NC—the adjusted book value sits between $2.75 and $3.37 per share.
  • The Lightera Strategic Partnership: In July 2025, OCC partnered with Lightera (part of the multi-billion dollar Furukawa Electric Group). Lightera invested $2M for a 7.24% stake at $3.11/share. This partnership allows OCC to integrate Lightera’s high-end optical solutions into its own portfolio, providing a clear path to penetrate the data center market more deeply.
  • Qualitative “Smart Money” Signal: The accumulation of a position by billionaire contrarian investor Jeffrey Gendell suggests a deep-value opportunity that quantitative screens might miss.

Catalysts

  • The “Bullet-Dodge” Theory: Recent stock price volatility following the December earnings call may have been a “bullet-dodge” event. Management had a clear incentive to let the stock price dip ahead of January’s annual equity grant cycle to secure a lower strike price for insiders, suggesting they remain bullish on the long-term outlook despite cautious public commentary.
  • NOL Preservation & Potential Buyout: OCC holds significant Net Operating Losses (NOLs) which shield future profits from taxes. Management’s use of an NOL Rights Plan suggests a desire to utilize these tax shields before any potential change in control. Given the aging board of directors and the strategic alignment with Lightera, a future buyout remains a plausible exit strategy.

Financial Outlook

Incremental sales from the Lightera partnership and data center upsells could push annual revenue toward $100 million. If OCC achieves a modest valuation multiple similar to peers like RF Industries, there is a positive skew with potential upside exceeding 100% from current levels, while the real estate assets provide a firm “floor” for the investment.
