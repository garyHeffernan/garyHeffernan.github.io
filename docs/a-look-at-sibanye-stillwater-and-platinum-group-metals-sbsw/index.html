A look at Sibanye-Stillwater and Platinum Group Metals $SBSW

× Date: Thu, Jun 12, 2025

Introduction

“The cure for low prices is low prices” - Commodity Speculator, RIP. Cause of death: bled out waiting for the cure.

It’s a cliche, but there is a dose of explanatory power in that phrase that will help undergird my investment thesis for platinum group metals as a sector, and Sibanye-Stillwater (“SBSW”) in particular. The other large publicly traded platinum group metals miners (Northam Platinum, Impala Platinum and Valterra Platinum) are attractive, too and offer upside with less downside due to lower production costs and/or lower leverage than SBSW. In general, the higher the cost of production, the more “torque” the company’s profits have to rising commodity prices. Rising profits typically correlate with rising stock prices.

Overview

Sibanye-Stillwater is a metals and mining company. The primary focus of this writeup will be on the company’s exposure to platinum group metals (“PGMs”, mainly platinum, palladium, and rhodium). The company also has exposure to gold and other metals. The company operates in South Africa and the United States. South Africa has significant geopolitical risk, however all publicly traded PGM mining companies have exposure to South Africa, making this an unavoidable risk if you want to invest in equities in the sector.

From the image below you can see that over time, PGMs, particularly South African operations, are the main contributor to SBSW’s EBITDA. When PGM prices rose in 2020-2021 due to COVID-related supply shocks, SBSW’s EBITDA (and stock price) soared. Platinum and palladium prices have since corrected (although very recently, platinum prices have taken off, hence the urgency in getting this writeup out) and you can see the effects in the company’s results: 41% of depressed EBITDA in Q1 2025 came from the company’s South African gold operation, compared to 7% in 2021.

Demand

Commodity prices go up when there is an imbalance between supply and demand. New supply requires investment and time, making it easier to forecast than demand. That being said, it’s helpful to understand what PGMs are used for. My contention is that a misunderstanding of the demand picture has contributed to an opportunity in this stock.

The primary sources of demand are for use in automotive catalysts (catalytic converters) and jewelry. Automotive catalysts were 43% of platinum demand and 84% of palladium demand in 2024. Both internal combustion engine (“ICE”) and hybrid vehicles use catalysts with PGM content to reduce emissions. So, a sudden stop in ICE/Hybrid vehicle sales would be catastrophic to the investment thesis. And during an ebullient forecast for battery electric vehicle (“BEV”) growth, the market began to forecast the death of non-BEV vehicles, and incidentally, the death of PGMs. I think this misperception in demand is contributing to the investment opportunity in SBSW. And I think it is timely. In fact, according to the IEA, BEV sales in Europe and US markets slowed in 2024 to 0% and 9% year-over-year, respectively. This is after years of explosive growth in the early 2020s. While China is showing no signs of slowing, Europe and US markets offer the first hint that BEV demand forecasts might have been and continue to be overly optimistic. ICE /Hybrid vehicles, and thus demand for PGMs, might be here longer than anticipated just a few short years ago.

Supply

According to research at UBS, South African PGM producers have underspent by ~$18BN in capital expenditures in the last decade, leading to supply deficits in 10 of the last 11 years. As mentioned above, overoptimistic demand forecasts for BEVs have contributed to pessimistic PGM demand forecasts, leading to underinvestment in supply. As mentioned above, BEV demand forecasts have started to disappoint, leading to a risk that high BEV penetration won’t materialize. This presents a risk that the PGM miners won’t have the necessary supply ready and available to meet the market at current PGM prices.

According to the World Platinum Investment Council (“WPIC”), we are already in a supply deficit today. And the deficit is expected to persist through the forecast period ending in 2029. Palladium was in surplus in 2022, has been in deficit since, and is expected to remain in a deficit until 2028 where it shows a slight surplus. The outlook for future PGM production is a -0.9% and -1.3% CAGR from 2024e to 2029e for platinum and palladium, respectively. We are starting to see evidence of supply disruptions and this is reflected in mine restructurings. These are the conditions of a tight market.

According to WPIC, ~40% of global PGM mine supply is operating at or below the cash cost of production. For production to increase to meet demand, prices would have to rise. This creates a conundrum for the PGM equities since a rising commodity price correlates to a rising stock price, and vice versa. In effect, the stock market is telling PGM operators not to invest in new production. To make the point, consider the number of times you have seen the stock market reward executive teams (via a rising stock price) based on metrics unrelated to free cash flow per share. These situations usually happen during manias. A recent one was the streaming industry and the market’s focus on subscriber growth. Netflix was rewarded for growing subscribers, so the non-Netflix media companies started to focus on subscriber growth at all costs, ignoring the unpleasant truth that unprofitable subscribers make for a dodgy business proposition. Now consider what’s happening in the PGM mining markets. The stock market is sending a signal to management not to invest in future supply today, because each dollar is better spent on buying back shares or paying dividends than adding new supply. These short to medium-term distortions have a way of working themselves out over time. But it takes time. Eventually, when a supply shortage occurs from years of underinvestment, the price of the underlying commodity rises, and over time, incentivizes producers to bring on new supply, which completes the cycle and causes the commodity price to head back down towards the marginal cost of production.

Market Dynamics and Price Response

Unless you’ve successfully invested in commodities before, it might be hard to take the following leap. The price response of the physical commodity is a function of the supply / demand picture at a given point in time. If there is a shortage, the price of the commodity will get bid until it clears the market. We all intuitively understand this point when hailing an Uber / Lyft / Waymo at a particularly busy location or during unusually bad weather. The car service charges “surge pricing” and you’ll pay as much as you’re willing to tolerate to get where you need to go. As ride prices rise, more supply (drivers) arrive to capture the high ride prices, which eventually drives prices back down as supply / demand comes into better balance. Demand may also be destroyed in this type of scenario, think about taking the subway instead of hailing a ride. What’s different for mining companies is that the lead time to bring on new supply (new drivers arriving in the analogy) is >10 years according to WPIC. And demand destruction, which will certainly occur at a high enough price, can not occur from substitution alone (taking the subway in the analogy). Platinum and palladium are substitutes for one another in catalytic converters, but their prices are both likely to rise in a supply shortage. When the day comes that the PGM prices start rising (and in the case of platinum, that day might be today), it will be some time before we see a price response downward from increased supply, unlike ride-hailing surge pricing which dissipates in a matter of hours.

Recycling

PGM supply comes from both mining and recycling. Approximately 25% of supply of PGMs comes from recycling, of which catalytic converters make up the biggest portion. Recycling supply is depressed from peak levels. One reason relates to automotive production disruptions during the COVID era: if you tried to buy a car during that time, it was extremely difficult. Auto dealers added surcharges to car prices simply because they could. The result is that cars were driven for longer, leading to less scrapping. Less scrapping puts downward pressure on recycling volumes. This should create a condition of “latent supply” that will return to the market should the economics make sense. Industry reports suggest recycling is not economic at prevailing prices. PGM prices are depressed today, but should that change, this represents a source of latent supply.

Valuation / Upside

A price target is the hardest thing to quantify, and I know that will make many members uncomfortable. In this case, I’d point you to recent history. If PGM prices enter an extended upcycle driven by historic underinvestment, then the stock should do well. The stock hit $20 in 2021 when platinum and palladium prices hit $1,300/oz and $3,000/oz, respectively. Today, SBSW is at $7/share, and you can see from the first image that EBITDA is depressed. From the SA PGM statement of operations (SBSW’s most meaningful segment) below you can see that margins were absolutely crushed in 2024. Platinum has started to move sharply upwards to $1,250, while palladium remains depressed at $1,000. A period of sustained higher PGM prices will flow through to the bottom line. If my analysis of supply is correct, and there is little prospect for new supply additions in the face of a rising commodity price, then this stock could enter an extended upcycle, putting time on our side to further refine a valuation / price target.

Risks

- Economic downturn which hurts automotive demand

- Greater than anticipated recycling supply comes to market

- Mine closures at SBSW’s mines

- BEV demand is greater than anticipated

Catalyst

- Higher platinum and palladium prices, driven by years of underinvestment

- Higher platinum and palladium prices, driven by investors’ desire to “hide” from inflationary government policies
