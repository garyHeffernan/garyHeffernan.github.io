Freerolling with Shell Midstream

× Date: Fri, Jul 1, 2022

In poker, a freeroll is a tournament with no entry free. By participating, your financial downside is zero, and the only downside is the opportunity cost of your time. Your upside is determined by how well you play (and some luck). It is the distribution of potential outcomes that makes a freeroll such a compelling investment idea, and it’s no surprise they rank amongst my favorites. Heads, I win. Tails, I don’t lose (too much).

Shell Midstream (ticker: ) is an equity that I think represents a freeroll at today’s price. Full disclosure: SHLX closed at $14.07 today; my cost basis is in the $13s.

What makes this a freeroll? SHLX is undergoing a transaction whereby its parent, Shell plc (ticker: ) has offered to acquire the SHLX units it currently does not own (SHEL currently owns ~70% of SHLX) at $12.89/unit. Math majors will notice that $12.89 represents 8% downside to today’s closing price; not exactly a freeroll. The math is correct, but assuming the deal closes (more on this later), consider the following: between now and the anticipated close, an investor today receives one $0.30 distribution per SHLX unit. That reduces your cost basis to $14.07 - $0.30 or $13.77. Your downside to the deal price is now 6.4%. Still not free, but it’s looking better. How do we improve upon this? This is where deal mechanics and precedent transactions play a key role.

Because SHLX is structured as an MLP, the take-private offer from SHEL isn’t what you would typically see in a situation involving a publicly traded company, or C-Corp. With a C-Corp, any acquirer would offer a premium price to get shareholders and the board of directors to agree to the acquisition. This is not required in an MLP takeover transaction; the general partner (in this case, SHEL), can legally acquire its subsidiary MLP at any price of its choosing. And you will notice that the offer price of $12.89/unit was done at a 0% premium to SHLX’s last closing price before the deal was announced.

It is my contention that the initial, 0% premium offer by SHEL is simply an opening move. A deal tactic. Why do I think this? Because now that the offer has been announced, a special committee of outsiders has been appointed by SHEL to evaluate the deal. Their job is to negotiate on behalf of the minority (non-SHEL) unitholders. Yes, they were appointed by SHEL and could simply do SHEL’s bidding. I am not that cynical. I think the special committee members work for themselves and care about their reputations. Ultimately, they do not wish to be sued for harming minority unitholders, who in this case are primarily retail investors (MLPs are marketed to yield-seeking retail investors). My base case is that the special committee will evaluate the deal and come up with a fair price for both SHEL and SHLX.

Precedent transactions are on my side. Historically, when an MLP take-private transaction of this kind is announced, the initial offer is raised by at least 15%. A 15% increase to the initial offer would result in 7.5% upside from today’s close if you include the upcoming $0.30 distribution slated to be paid in early August. A 15% increase represents my base case.

There is also potential for a much higher deal price. SHEL’s offer was announced in February of this year. At the time of the announcement, the oil price was lower and the outlook for oil and gas had not reached the fever pitch that we see today. Further, oil majors like SHEL invest on multi-decade time horizons. SHEL’s acquisition comes at a time when certain refined product and offshore pipelines are under-earning. Giving SHLX full credit for its under-earning assets could see a deal price of $20/unit. Recall, SHLX went public at $23/unit in 2014 when oil prices were ~$80/bbl vs. ~$100/bbl today. A revised price to $20/unit plus the $0.30 distribution equates to 44% upside.

One final point. My analysis hinges on the deal closing. I think it’s almost a certain thing—there are no guarantees in investing!—that the deal closes. Why? Because SHLX is just a tax structure. SHEL already owns and operates these assets. The acquisition is simply the undoing of a complicated financial transaction that was done for tax purposes. The tax benefits no longer exist, and it makes sense for SHEL to bring the ~30% of SHLX it does not own back under the SHEL umbrella. There are no regulatory reviews, no financing risk, no shareholder votes, etc. If SHEL wants to close the deal, they will, and I can’t think of anything that would stop them at this point.

In summary, the only way I see this investment losing money is if SHEL does not raise their initial offer by more than 6.8%. I think the odds of this happening are less than 5%. As for the shape of the remaining distribution: it’s unknowable. Precedent transactions suggest initial offers are raised by at least 15%, and in some cases, >100% (see Hoegh LNG). Heads, I win. Tails, I don’t lose (too much!).
