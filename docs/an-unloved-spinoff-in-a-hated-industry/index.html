An unloved spinoff in a hated industry

× Date: Sat, May 17, 2025

Until this month, Starz was part of Lionsgate Entertainment, but it is now its own publicly traded company due to its separation on May 7. The separation created a compelling asymmetric opportunity to buy an unloved, under-followed and potentially misunderstood company. Starz operates in a hated industry, but its business quality is higher than its closest publicly traded peer. The shares offer limited downside, and a re-rating to peer multiples offers upside. The shares are trading at the low-end of its deserved valuation and could be worth as much as >100% more depending on business execution and the market’s reception to this new issue. The company intends to deleverage in the future, offering further equity upside if they’re successful. Finally, the company is an attractive acquisition target should the industry commence a highly-anticipated consolidation cycle.

Background

In the past, Starz was a premium add-on service to cable television packages. It has been well known for a decade that cable television cord-cutting is real and threatens business models that rely on the cable television ecosystem. Starz was no exception and after receiving a private market valuation of $4.4BN when it was sold to Lionsgate in 2016, it now trades in the public market at an $825MM valuation, an 81% loss in value.

Today, 70% of Starz’s revenue is an add-on service to a digital TV package. For example, you can go to Amazon Prime Video and select a Starz tile as an additional service. When I went to do that today, I could get Starz at an introductory rate of $2.99/month for two months then $10.99/month thereafter. This common teaser model does prey on the laziness of the consumer, but Starz has kept domestic (U.S. and Canada) revenue flat year-over-year in an industry that is perceived to be shrinking at a double-digit rate which suggests persistence to their business model. The future of the TV video business will be one where technology behemoths like Amazon and Google become the new distribution companies (basically the new cable companies) with Prime Video and YouTube as the digital distribution mechanisms. For a content programmer to survive in this new world, they will need to have transitioned away from linear distribution toward digital distribution. With 70% of its revenue coming from digital today, Starz both 1) successfully pivoted to the new digital ecosystem and 2) demonstrates that consumers will pay for worthwhile content.

Publicly Traded Peers

The closest publicly traded cable television video peer is AMC Networks (“AMCX”). AMCX has a $1.9BN enterprise value and a $280MM market cap. Leverage is 2.9x on a consolidated net basis. Both revenue and EBITDA have declined at a double-digit rate in the most recent reporting year. AMCX is a larger company than Starz, but its business mix is more exposed to linear cord-cutting with only 28% of revenue from digital streaming. As well, AMCX also generates revenue from linear television advertising; Starz does not. This high-margin revenue stream is especially painful to lose when subscribers are declining as they are today. Given AMCX’s business profile and linear television headwinds, it trades at 5.21x NTM EV/EBITDA. Given Starz’s higher digital exposure, pure-play digital subscription business and growth potential, I believe it deserves no less than AMCX’s multiple, the implications of which will be discussed below.

Separation Transaction

The answer to “why might this be mispriced” is that, in addition to being in a hated industry, the company has just recently separated from Lionsgate Entertainment and began trading under the ticker “STRZ” on May 7th of this year. The company will host its first business update call after market close on May 29th. In the meantime, the only available financials are from the spin-off materials, which leave some room for ambiguity, particularly around corporate allocations. In discussing the financials I want to discuss both what management forecasts for EBITDA as well as a conservative estimate of EBITDA from assessing the spin-off financials.

Starz CEO Jeffrey Hirsch claims the company operates with a 15% profit margin and he is targeting 20% in the near future. Profit is not clearly defined, but I assume it means EBITDA. Starz’s revenue run-rate is $1.4bn, so a 15% EBITDA margin would imply $210MM of EBITDA. On the same revenue base, 20% EBITDA margins would imply $280MM in EBITDA. By checking these numbers with the spin-off materials, it’s not clear how corporate overhead and SBC are factoring into Hirsch’s numbers. If you allocate old-Lionsgate’s corporate overhead and SBC pro-rata by revenue, EBITDA could be burdened by an additional $50MM, giving us a range of $160-230MM in EBITDA.

5.2x EBITDA at the low end is supported by AMCX’s multiple as discussed above. 7x EBITDA as a high end take-private multiple is in the realm of possibility, should the industry begin a highly-anticipated wave of consolidation. That puts the downside valuation at $832MM ($160MM * 5.2x) which converts to a price target of $13, flat to Friday’s closing price. If I’m being too conservative and CEO Hirsch is correct that EBITDA is $210MM today, then 5.2x * $210MM = $1,092MM, which is a price target of $29 or +122%. Applying take-out premiums, should Starz be acquired in a larger industry consolidation wave, provides even more upside to the above numbers.

Risks

Given the subscription model, the company is exposed to churn (which today is high by industry standards). Mitigations include increased bundling to retain customers and a more efficient content windowing strategy.

The company has content agreements with its former studio, Lionsgate, which expire in 2028. The loss of popular content and/or the prospect of more expensive content costs post-2028 poses risk to the business.

The company carries significant debt.

Catalyst

  • Execution

  • The presentation of clear, stand-alone financials

  • Increased market recognition
