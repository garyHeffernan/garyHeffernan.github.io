2023: The Year of the Lion(sgate)

× Date: Sat, Dec 31, 2022

Lions Gate Entertainment Corp. ($LGF-B) at $5.43 is an asymmetric long opportunity representing multi-bagger upside and limited downside over a 12-18 month time horizon. The stock has been left for dead after management failed to deliver on their promise to secure an investment in the company. Lions Gate is cheap on a sum-of-the-parts basis, which I believe to be well-known by the market. The investment opportunity arises from the sentiment surrounding the stock, which I think is perfectly captured by this Don Coxe quote: “The most exciting returns are to be had from an asset class where those who know it best, love it least, because they have been hurt the most.” Anyone familiar with this stock knows that it is a perennial acquisition target and a deal never materializes. Now is the right time to bet on a potential deal in the near future because the stock is pricing in no chance of success on an eventual sale of one or both its business units, despite management’s statements in regulatory filings to the contrary. Insider purchases have been significant, notably in the last two years and especially recently. What is the stock worth if a spin-off occurs in-line with management’s stated timeline of a company separation by September 2023? In a blue sky case I think the shares are worth $40 (>7x). In a downside case I think the shares are worth $12 (>2x). Until the separation occurs, the stock could be dead money and languish alongside the broader indices and media stocks in what is shaping up to be a vicious bear market.

Business Overview

LGF contains two different businesses that will be separated by September of 2023. One is the Studio business, the other, Starz.

The Studio

The crown jewel within the Studio segment is a film and television library with over 17,000 titles. Management has been investing in the library, successfully growing LTM revenues to 747Mtodayfrom 300M in 2017. Library revenue has 50% cash flow margins. Revenue is driven by both price and volume. An example will help illustrate the pricing power in the library. In 2011, Netflix paid $1M/episode for the rights to stream Mad Men. When that contract expired and came up for renewal a decade later, Amazon paid $3M/episode, or 3x. Obviously, not all content increases in value like that, but it’s important to keep in mind that time is on the side of high quality content. LGF retained the rights to nearly all of their content, allowing them to supply product to the highest bidder each time it comes up for renewal.

Volume is driven by new content that is added to the library, either via M&A or organically. The company is no stranger to M&A, having acquired a handful of studios over the years. Organically, new titles are produced in LGF’s motion picture production business, which is another part of the Studio segment. I won’t spend much time going over the motion picture business since I give it no credit in my valuation as its value is tightly wound together with the library. In short, it’s a low-margin, hit-driven business that has been negatively impacted by Covid. Management has done a good job structuring deals to lay-off risk from any single bad production. Lastly, the Studio contains a diversified, scripted TV content business whose revenue is lumpy, but produces $1B in revenue and $100M in segment profit, on average. TV series are sold to paid-TV networks, subscription video on demand (SVOD) and advertising-based video on demand (AVOD) services, and the timing of revenue is dependent on when deals get signed.

Starz

Lions Gate purchased Starz for $4.4B in 2016. The company was historically a linear-first, streaming-second, premium subscription service, but that has changed with streaming now accounting for 71% of Starz subscribers and 62% of its revenues.

What the market misunderstands about Starz is that the losses from the business are a reflection of heavy international investment by the management team. The domestic business is already profitable and has been for some time. On the most recent earnings call, management finally signaled their intent to stem the losses on the international side. Here’s the company:

*“We’ve made a strategic decision to exit 7 international territories, 6 markets in Continental Europe as well as Japan. This process has already begun and will be completed by the end of the fiscal year.” *

and

“We expect … our total international subscriber base at year end to exceed 7 million. We expect International segment profit will improve significantly on a quarter-over-quarter basis, starting with the current December quarter. We remain committed to reaching break-even in our international business exiting calendar year 2024 or earlier.”

At quarter-end, Starz International had 14.8M subscribers. This number excludes 2.0M Starz Arabia subscribers. Management just guided to >7M international subscribers as a result of the restructuring, or a ~50% cut. This is management waiving the white flag on growing international subscribers at all costs. Management is now serious about profitability over growth.

Okay, so Starz, which has been a perennial money-loser for the company due to international investments, has finally committed to stop burning money internationally. When international operates at break-even, the profitability from the U.S. segment can shine through.

How did we get here?

Back to the combined company and its languishing stock price. LGF.B shares started the year at approximately $16 and have basically been in a downtrend since. During the summer, the shares bounced in anticipation of management delivering on their promise to sign a deal for an investment in the company. In September, When the market rightly concluded that no deal was forthcoming, the shares resumed their downtrend and haven’t stopped. With no near-term catalysts on the horizon, the stock has been subjected to relentless selling pressure, hitting levels last seen during the March 2020 market crash precipitated by the Covid panic. This difficult to value business that just delivered a big disappointment to investors presents an incredible buying opportunity for contrarian-minded, value investors.

Where do we go from here?

In late 2021, the company announced their intentions to spin Starz and retain the Studio as the parent. An investment in one or both of the businesses was expected alongside the separation. The company changed plans on September 28, 2022 when they announced that they now intend to spin the Studio and retain Starz as the parent. The purpose of splitting the companies is to reveal that the sum of the parts is greater than the whole, so the big picture suggests that it doesn’t matter which entity gets spun. Retaining Starz as the parent allows the company to retain its $1B of low-coupon debt at Starz without having to refinance due to a change in control. More on that later.

It is my understanding that Starz and the Studio are going to be separated in a way that is taxable to the corporate parent but tax-free to shareholders. How is that possible? One way that is possible is if LGF orchestrates a Morris Trust or Reverse Morris Trust transaction. These transactions are basically spin-offs with concurrent investments in either the parent (Morris Trust) or SpinCo (Reverse Morris Trust) entity. For these transactions to qualify as tax-free to the corporate parent, the investment by the third party cannot be for control, i.e. the investment has to be for less than 50% of the entity. However, it is my understanding that a third party can make a greater than 50% investment and still make the transaction tax-free to the shareholders. This would allow LGF to concurrently separate the businesses and have one of them immediately acquired. This is pure speculation on my part, but it is supported by the current fact pattern at the company. Why do I say this? Well the transaction has taken longer than expected. The delay is forgivable in the light of the company having to orchestrate not just a spin-off, but a merger at the same time as a spin-off. A company like Lions Gate, that licenses its owned intellectual property, probably has a lot of legal work to do to get everything lined up. And since LGF sells content to Starz, inter-company agreements need to be worked out ahead of both a deal and a separation. Getting all of the pieces to line up just right isn’t easy, especially as the debt markets freeze up and the stock market sells off. If LGF announces a Morris Trust or Reverse Morris Trust transaction sometime before the Form 10 is filed in March, then that right there represents a catalyst that is not being priced in by the market, a free option if you will. Should this free option end up “in the money”, we would get a valuation marker on one or both of the businesses ahead of the spin-off transaction. This would immediately re-rate the shares higher. Like I said, I’m not counting on it, but I don’t think anyone else is either, which adds to the positively-skewed asymmetry in the shares.

The Importance of Competent Insiders

As previously mentioned, LGF is a perennial M&A target, but a deal never materializes for the company. In fact, it was rumored that the company turned down a $40/share offer from Hasbro in 2018. Recall the shares trade with a $5 handle now. Yikes. So why do I think LGF is finally going to be sold?

Part of it has to do with the involvement of Gordon “Gordy” Crawford. Take a moment to read about his background here if you aren’t familiar with him. He’s a legendary media investor with half a century of experience. At Capital Group, his job required him to get to know all of the media executives better than anyone else in the business. It’s no surprise that out of all the people in the world, it was Gordy who was hanging out at Ted Turner’s ranch when Turner Broadcasting agreed to be acquired by Time Warner.

Gordy has been with Lions Gate since he retired from Capital Group in 2013. It’s been almost a decade since he has been on LGF’s board. So why has Gordy been buying shares of LGF in the open market, hand over fist? His official title on the board is co-chair of the strategic advisory committee. Given his Hollywood connections he probably thinks he’s going to be able to help get a deal done for this company. I don’t think someone with an impeccable investment background is interested in throwing away money. I think he knows what we know, which is that the sum of the parts is greater than the whole, and that the market isn’t giving the company any credit for it. He knows that selling the company and/or splitting it up will help unlock value, and he’s in a unique position to create that outcome. Gordy’s $11.6M worth of open market purchases in 2021 and 2022 dwarf all prior year purchases. He would not start buying shares like he has been if he thought the company would remain a value trap forever.

This is backed up by purchases from the other insiders as well. Since the 8-k came out on 9/28 that sent the stock crashing on the news that no deal had been found, insiders purchased $8.5M worth of stock, or 37% of all insider purchases since 2020 by value.

He Who Shall Not Be Named

Okay, let’s name him. Mark Rachesky. Deservedly or undeservedly, he seems to have a reputation for overplaying his hand and holding his positions past their sell-by dates. Rachesky runs MHR, a hedge fund based out of New York. He has been on the board for over a decade. One of the bear cases on the stock is that Rachesky will never sell. Here’s why I think that doesn’t make any sense. Why would Mark agree to separate Starz and the Studio if he had no intention of selling one or both of the businesses? Rachesky is Chairman of the Board, and board approval is required to complete a corporate action such as a spin-off. The company would not issue an 8-k, a regulatory filing with the SEC, saying that it was contemplating a spin-off if it did not have board approval. So Rachesky is okay with the separation transaction, which is something that is a precursor to a sale of one or both of the businesses.

The other thing about MHR is that it does not have as much control as you might think. Rachesky owns a lot of the A shares, or what are known as the voting shares. The market misperceives these shares to have super-voting rights in an M&A transaction. But they don’t. LGF is a Vancouver-based company, and A and B shares have equal voting rates in an M&A transaction. I’d go as far as saying Gordy’s influence, including the Capital Group stake and the Liberty stake, almost entirely offsets MHR’s votes. So if Gordy wants to sell and MHR wants to drag its feet, there’s a chance that Gordy could have his way and MHR couldn’t do anything about it. When it comes time to vote, who is going to “ride with Rachesky”? Not me.

Valuation

Netflix, the media bellwether, has been crushed alongside the broader market sell-off. Traditional media stocks have not fared any better. In my view this leads to the misperception that all M&A deals need to be massively marked down from prior transaction comparable levels. Comcast CEO Brian Roberts made the following point regarding Comcast’s view of Hulu’s value:

While Disney has pointed to the dip in Netflix’s market cap as an indicator for Hulu’s value, Comcast sees the streamer’s valuation tied to whatever Hulu would fetch as part of a competitive auction of the asset.

The stock market is one thing, competitive auctions are another, and although related, they are not the same thing.

Libraries—of which Lions Gate is one of two remaining large, independent libraries alongside Paramount—are arms-dealers that sell content to streaming platforms.

Streaming platform stocks have been punished in 2022, presumably for their large investments and murky economics. This has weighed on all media stocks, including LGF. If you believe that cord-cutting will continue, i.e. we are not going back to cable as the primary form of TV distribution, then you also believe in a world of streaming platforms, and bigger, more-consolidated platforms than those that exist today. My view is that cord-cutting isn’t complete, and won’t be complete until everything is delivered by subscription video on demand (SVOD) or advertising-based video on demand (AVOD). Until we reach stasis in the streamers’ business models–which we are nowhere near, just see how many households still haven’t cut the cord, or how much streamers are still investing in new content, despite the growth rate slowing–then content will hold its value, and potentially increase in value as rights are renegotiated and streamers acquire content. An arms-dealer like Lions Gate is well-positioned to make money in that environment.

Transaction Multiples

Using management’s newly-issued guidance, the Studio business is expected to generate a range of 420−470M in EBITDA for fiscal year 2024. Based on transaction comparables, the studio is worth 15x at a minimum in a takeover scenario or 6.3−7.0B. LGF is one of the few remaining studios that could provide a significant presence in Hollywood, particularly at a time when access to high-quality video content is highly valuable. The company’s library, development opportunities, and talent relationships make it a unique and highly valuable asset. Previous examples of studios being sold for similar valuations include Dreamworks, which was acquired by Comcast for 17x EBITDA in 2016 and MGM, which was acquired by Amazon for 20x EBITDA in 2021.

Starz’s earnings power is slightly more complicated, which I will address in a section further below. Even without giving any credit to Starz, transaction multiples at the Studio make this a compelling investment opportunity.

Dreamworks

The Dreamworks acquisition provides a telling example of what a studio is worth in a take-private transaction. There are two points worth considering: 1) Comcast acquired Dreamworks in 2016, well-before the recent stock-market mania that peaked at the end of 2021 and 2) the acquisition was >100% of the then-current comparable trading valuations.

Dreamworks was acquired for $41 per share in cash. Look at the ranges above. In some cases, using market-implied valuations, the take-private price of $41/share was >5x what market multiples implied. This example illustrates how poor of a job the market can do in valuing rare assets like studio business, and also shows how it’s important not to get anchored to recent trading prices when valuing these kinds of businesses. The share price may bounce around, and languish at times, but when it comes time to sell, the valuations are high for these kinds of businesses. Using the Dreamworks multiple, the business is worth $12.3B, which is 4.1x LTM Studio revenue of $3B. This represents the blue-sky case and would be an unbelievable outcome.

Hello Sunshine

In 2019, Reese Witherspoon’s scripted content business Hello Sunshine was valued at $900M. Lions Gate’s diversified scripted content business has >30 scripted series that get sold to paid-TV networks, SVOD and now the growing AVOD (Pluto, Tubi, Roku) market. It does between 80−100M in segment profit, which fluctuates depending on when deals get signed, and should be worth 10x profit or at least as much as Hello Sunshine, or 800M−1B.

Margin of Safety

The valuation of the entire company today is underpinned just by the library alone. You can get to a $4B valuation easily on $375M of cash flow. This is one of my favorite things about the investment in LGF at today’s prices–you’re covered by a scarce, valuable asset that can be sold once it is separated from Starz in 2023.

What is Starz Worth on a standalone basis?

The company expects Starz FY 2024 EBITDA to be 175−225M. The important thing here is the timing of international hitting break-even. Management expects this to occur in the final quarter of 2024, so there will be losses–albeit shrinking ones–up to that point. This depresses Starz FY24 EBITDA. On a run-rate basis, I estimate Starz can generate $300M in EBITDA. At 7x EBITDA for Starz, that’s a $2.1B business, which is significantly below the company’s internal estimate for the business of $2.9B. Recall Starz was acquired for $4.4B and in the most recent quarter, management wrote it down by $1.48B. There is a chance I am being too conservative in appraising its value.

What about all that debt at Starz?

Issuing $1B of debt at a 5.5% coupon in 2021 and terming it out to 2029 ended up creating a hidden asset for Starz. The $1B liability is now worth less than what it is marked at on the balance sheet. At ~60 cents on the dollar, Starz can opportunistically repurchase debt at a discount to face between now and 2029 and deleverage the balance sheet. If I were a private equity firm interested in Starz, I would make an equity investment to provide Starz with cash to do exactly that. Starz’s equity value would re-rate awfully quickly.

Potential Momentum at the Studio

The television business has been running on all cylinders at Lions Gate while motion picture revenue has been stagnant due to a lack of franchise hit releases. That is about to change in the calendar year 2023. In the next year we will see releases of some of Lions Gate’s biggest franchises, including John Wick, The Hunger Games, and Saw.

Buzz from a successful roll-out should support short-term momentum and sentiment in the stock. It’s possible that management is going to use this boost in sentiment to sail off the sunset and sell the Studio business to the highest bidder, but that is pure speculation on my part.

Aside from short-term buzz and a boost to the stock price, the film slate that Lions Gate is releasing has significant cash flow potential, not just from the films themselves, but from the revenue boost they will receive from all prior films in the same franchise. Here’s the company at a recent investor presentation:

“When we released John Wick 3, the revenue ultimates on John Wick 1 and 2 increased by … a couple hundred million dollars of additional revenue. We won’t keep it all, partners, talent participate. When you take that and you understand that. And what that can mean at a company of Lions gates scale and size. Look forward at Wick, Saw, Hunger Games, and on and on … and the rest of business hitting on all cylinders. Think we’re set up in a really strong way to benefit from coming out of this pandemic.”

John Wick 1 and 2 did $200M at the box office, which I will use as a proxy for how big of a hit the film is and its revenue potential. When John Wick 3 was released, it did $327M at the box office. This film was obviously a hit for the company, outdoing the prior two releases. What surprised me was that the action of releasing John Wick 3 added $200M more in revenue to the prior two films. By the way, John Wick 4 is being released in March 2023 with a spin-off film called Ballerina featuring Ana de Armas in the works.

What about the other big franchises? Hunger Games has done $3B at the box office so far over four films. That’s 10x the size of the John Wick franchise, pre John-Wick 3. What could that mean for revenue potential for the prior four Hunger Games films as viewers re-watch that content? Another $2B for the franchise? What about Saw, another film that comes out in 2023 and has done $1B at the box office. I don’t know how to estimate this. But I do know that releasing sequels increases the value of the previously-released films in the same franchise. The market is sleeping on what could be an explosive source of revenue upside for the company once these films hit the screens.

Why is the share price so low?

Management failed to get an investment in either of its businesses and took too long to separate them. I think the market lost a lot of faith in management, which has contributed to the low stock price. There are a few things that argue in management’s favor when considering why they have taken so long to announce a deal.

  1. The debt markets froze up, and “deal stocks”–stocks whose next known catalyst was a corporate action like M&A or outside investment–got crushed as event-driven traders moved onto greener pastures.
  2. Transaction complexity has extended the timeline. Management announced the separation transaction will be tax-free to shareholders but potentially taxable to the parent. Needless to say, getting all the ducks in a row on this transaction is not trivial and requires coordination across multiple stakeholders.
  3. Library rights cadence. For the library to have value, the rights have to be sold to someone. The library content is sold for a set duration, and then comes up for renewal. If someone was interested in buying the library to hoard the content for themselves, they might prefer to have some portion of the library unencumbered. It is conceivable that a buyer is waiting for the right time–when key pieces of content come off of contract–to strike.
  4. Management wants to extract maximum value in a sale and is waiting for the right moment to sell. As discussed above, Lions Gate is releasing a slate of hits in 2023. These hits could help management in negotiating the value of an investment in either business or an M&A transaction for the entire company.

Conclusion

The company is cheap on a sum-of-the-parts basis, and a separation transaction will occur next year, allowing the market to better value those parts. Businesses like Lions Gate aren’t always valued well by the market, and sometimes it takes M&A to unlock value for shareholders. The company could be worth up to $12B or $40 in a blue sky scenario. Downside is protected by the library, and various transaction multiples support multi-bagger stock prices at various points between today’s price at $40/share. LGF is a highly asymmetric stock, and I don’t think you need to know exactly at what price it will be taken-private, you just have to know that your downside is protected, and the upcoming separation process is a catalyst toward unlocking value. Heavy insider buying corroborates this view.
