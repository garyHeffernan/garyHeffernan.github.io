Paramount's Path to Avoid the $7B Termination Fee

× Date: Tue, Aug 18, 2026

The market expects that Paramount would owe Warner Bros. Discovery $7B if it does not close their merger on account of the lawsuit brought by the State AGs & the WGA. There is a path for Paramount to owe Warner Bros. Discovery no fee, which I will argue below. This is not a prediction; it is a scenario analysis that I don’t see covered elsewhere.

Under Which Conditions does the Termination Fee Apply?

The $7B Termination Fee applies if Paramount does not close the merger by March 4, 2027, subject to one automatic extension to June 4, 2027, on account of failing to secure regulatory approvals or the presence of a governmental order prohibiting the transaction from closing.

All Regulatory Approvals Are Secured

On August 14, 2026, Paramount issued a press release with the following statement: Paramount Skydance has satisfied all regulatory clearances required under the merger agreement to close its proposed acquisition of Warner Bros. Discovery.

With all regulatory approvals in-hand, the only outstanding item prohibiting the transaction from closing is the “No-Close Order.” It is also the only outstanding item that could trigger the termination fee as well as the automatic extension to June 4, 2027.

Is the No-Close Order an Injunction?

Paramount filed a motion yesterday arguing that their agreement not to close the merger, or the “No-Close Order,” is an “injunction” and should be classified as such by the court.

The No-Close Order is an agreement made between Paramount and the Plaintiffs (State AGs & WGA) not to close the merger of Paramount and Warner Bros. Discovery by as late as June 2027, pending the outcome of a trial on the merits of the case brought by the Plaintiffs.

The reason that Paramount is arguing that the No-Close Order is an injunction is to force the Plaintiffs to post a bond to cover the damages Paramount believes they are owed should they succeed at trial. Paramount’s argument is that an antitrust law, specifically the Clayton Act, requires Plaintiffs to post bond in this scenario. Paramount believes the bond should cover expected damages, which they estimate to be at least $1.88B, the same amount as the ticking fee payment owed to Warner Bros. Discovery shareholders.

To force the Plaintiffs to post bond, Paramount needs the court to find the No-Close Order an injunction per the Clayton Act, an antitrust law. It follows that a finding that the No-Close Order is an injunction is a finding that the No-Close Order is a governmental order under antitrust law.

The judge will ultimately determine whether or not the No-Close Order is a governmental order under antitrust law.

If the judge rules the No-Close Order is not an injunction under the Clayton Act, it does not satisfy the condition of being a “governmental order as a result of any antitrust law”, and therefore does not trigger the Termination Fee Payable by PSKY to WBD.
